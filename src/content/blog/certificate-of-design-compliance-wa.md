Your builder says the permit needs a certificate of design compliance. The council says it needs an engineer's certificate. You are holding one quote and wondering whether you have just been asked to pay for the same piece of paper twice. You haven't, but the two documents are easy to mix up, and getting them in the wrong order is one of the more common ways a Perth permit application stalls.

This guide sets out who signs what in a Western Australian building permit application, where a structural engineer fits, and what the government charges on the way through. It is general information about the rules as we understand them, not advice on your job.

## What a certificate of design compliance actually is

Under the Building Act 2011 (WA), the certificate of design compliance is the building surveyor's statement that the plans for the proposed work comply with the building standards. In council paperwork it is often called the BA3.

The person who signs it is a building surveyor. It is not signed by the owner, the builder or the structural engineer. That is the first thing to get straight.

A structural engineer's work sits underneath it. Section 16 of the Act says a certified application must include the certificate of design compliance signed by a building surveyor, the plans it specifies, and each technical certificate the surveyor relied on. The engineer's certificate is one of those supporting documents. The surveyor reads it, decides whether to rely on it, and then signs their own certificate.

So the document the engineer signs and the document the surveyor signs are different pieces of paper with different jobs. Neither replaces the other.

## Who signs the certificate of design compliance: certified or uncertified routes

WA has two application routes, and they change who signs the certificate.

In a certified application (the BA1 route), you or your builder engages a registered private building surveying contractor. That surveyor signs the certificate of design compliance and lodges it with the application. City of Rockingham's own explainer describes it this way.

In an uncertified application (the BA2 route), you lodge the plans with the local government and the permit authority refers them to a building surveyor. That surveyor decides whether to sign the certificate of design compliance, and the council issues it with the permit. Uncertified applications are only available for Class 1a and Class 10 buildings, which covers most houses and their sheds, carports and patios. Bigger or more complex jobs go the certified route.

Your local government is the permit authority for a house either way. It assesses the application and issues the permit.

## Where the structural engineer signs

A building surveyor is not expected to check a steel beam or a slab design from scratch. That is why the Act allows them to rely on a technical certificate, defined in section 3 as a certificate signed by a specialist. For structural work, that specialist is usually a structural engineer.

Here is roughly how the paperwork stacks up.

| Document | Who signs it | What it says | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Certificate of design compliance (BA3) | Building surveyor | The plans comply with the building standards | With the permit application, or issued with the permit on the uncertified route |
| Technical certificate | A specialist, often a structural engineer | The structural design is adequate for the work described | Attached to the application; the surveyor may rely on it |
| Council structural certificate form | The certifying engineer | Site class, design wind speed, and the list of drawings covered | The council's own form, where it asks for one |
| Building permit | The permit authority (local government) | Permission to carry out the building work | Held by the owner or builder |

The council form row is worth a closer look. City of Perth's form F0162 is titled "Certificate of Structural Sufficiency". The signatory states the site class under AS 2870, the design gust wind speed, and the documents covered, and confirms they are a "Professional Engineer" as defined in the Building Code of Australia. Other councils use their own names and layouts.

That is why "certificate of structural adequacy" is not a statutory WA form. The Act's term is "technical certificate". If you want the long version of that naming problem, we cover it in [certificate of structural adequacy in WA](/blog/certificate-of-structural-adequacy-wa/).

### A registration point that applies from mid-2027

Registration of structural building engineers began on 1 July 2024, with a transition period until 1 July 2027. After that date, structural engineering work must be done by a registered building engineer, and unregistered building engineering work is an offence. Some older pages still say 1 July 2026. That date has been superseded.

"Engineer" and "structural engineer" are not reserved titles in WA, so the words alone tell you nothing. You can check a person or firm against the Building Engineer Register kept by Building and Energy (DEMIRS). The [WA Government's building engineering registration guide](https://www.wa.gov.au/government/multi-step-guides/building-engineering-registration) explains the scheme.

## Three ways this plays out on a real job

These are anonymised scenarios, built to show how the paperwork works. They are not case studies and the figures are illustrations, not quotes.

### A Baldivis gable patio the council will not permit without an engineer's certificate

A family in Baldivis wants a roofed gable patio. The council will not issue a permit without an engineer's certificate showing the structure is designed for the site.

The exemption rules matter here. Under the Building Regulations 2012, a freestanding Class 10a structure is only exempt from a permit if its floor area is not more than 10 square metres, it is no higher than 2.4 metres, and it is not in wind region C or D. A pergola is exempt up to 20 square metres, but the regulations define a pergola as a structure that does not have a roof. A roofed patio is therefore not a pergola under that item.

Once a permit is needed, the engineer's certificate goes in as a technical certificate, and the surveyor decides whether to rely on it. We cover the patio side in [engineering certificates for patios and sheds](/blog/engineering-certificate-patio-shed/), and the service page is [patio and shed certificates](/patio-shed-certificates/).

A patio like this might carry an estimated value of $15,000. On the certified route the permit fee is 0.19 per cent of value, but not less than $121.00, so the minimum applies. The Building Services Levy is $61.65 for work at or under $45,000. The engineer's fee is separate and quoted per job.

### A Duncraig home where the owners want the kitchen wall gone

A 1970s double-brick home in Duncraig has a wall between the kitchen and the living area. The owners want it removed for an open-plan layout.

Under the same regulations, removing or altering an element that contributes to the support of another element is not exempt work. So a load-bearing wall removal needs a permit. Whether this particular wall is load-bearing is something only an engineer looking at the house can tell you, and we will not guess from here. Our page on [wall removal engineering](/wall-removal-engineering/) explains what an engineer typically looks at.

Here the engineer designs the beam and prepares the details, and signs them. The building surveyor then relies on that technical certificate when deciding whether to sign the certificate of design compliance. The owners do not need to chase the surveyor for a beam calculation, but the engineer's documents need to be in the application in a form the surveyor can use.

At an estimated $12,000 of work, the permit fee would again sit at the $121.00 minimum. The beam design fee is not published anywhere for Perth, so it is quoted after the engineer sees photos, plans or the site.

### A Bibra Lake family adding a second storey

A Bibra Lake family plans a second storey on a 1980s brick-and-tile house. Any change to floor area or height takes the work outside the permit exemption, so there is no question about whether a permit is needed.

The question is what the surveyor needs from the engineer. City of Rockingham's uncertified-application checklist asks for structural engineer's details "designed and certified by a Practising Structural Engineer". What an engineer must check on an existing house for a second storey, such as the existing walls and footings, is not something we can state as a rule. It depends on the house and the design.

Say the estimated value of the work is $180,000. This is an illustration only. On the certified route the permit fee would be 0.19 per cent, or $342. On the uncertified route it would be 0.32 per cent, or $576. The Building Services Levy is 0.137 per cent because the value is over $45,000, about $246.60. The Construction Training Fund levy is 0.2 per cent of the value of construction work including GST, but only where the work is over $100,000, so it would be about $360.

## Timeframes and fees on the permit itself

The permit authority has a fixed period to decide, set by the Building Regulations 2012. For an uncertified application it is 25 business days, and for a certified application it is 10 business days, unless you agree a longer period in writing. The clock stops when the authority asks for further information. City of Rockingham publishes the same 10 and 25 day figures.

These periods cover the authority's decision. They say nothing about how long an engineer takes to produce drawings and a certificate. No Perth firm we found publishes that turnaround, and it varies with the job. Ask each engineer for their own timing in writing.

Here are the government charges that sit around the certificate. All figures are from the current regulations and the wa.gov.au approvals page, and value of work is taken including GST.

| Charge | Rate |
|---|---|
| Certified building permit, Class 1 or Class 10 | 0.19% of estimated value, not less than $121.00 |
| Uncertified building permit | 0.32% of estimated value, not less than $121.00 |
| Building approval certificate, unauthorised work | 0.38% of estimated value, minimum $121.00 |
| Building Services Levy, permit | 0.137% if over $45,000; $61.65 if $45,000 or less |
| Construction Training Fund levy | 0.2% of construction value, only where the work is over $100,000 |

Watch for stale numbers. Some council fee sheets still show a $110.00 minimum and a $20,000 levy threshold. Those are out of date. The current minimum is $121.00 and the levy threshold is $100,000.

Engineers' fees are the missing line. No Perth engineering firm we checked publishes a price for a beam design, a crack report or a site-specific patio certificate. Engineers quote each job, and the price depends on the house, the structure and what the council needs. Treat any single figure for "an engineer's certificate" with suspicion unless it tells you exactly what it covers.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a structural engineer sign the certificate of design compliance?

No. A building surveyor signs the certificate of design compliance. The structural engineer signs a technical certificate or the council's own structural form, and the surveyor may rely on it. The two documents work together but they are different documents.

### How long does a WA building permit take once it is lodged?

For an uncertified application the authority has 25 business days, and for a certified application it has 10 business days, unless a longer period is agreed in writing. The clock stops if the authority asks for more information. Time spent on engineering and surveying beforehand comes on top of that.

### Is a certificate of structural adequacy the same as a certificate of design compliance?

No. A certificate of structural adequacy, sometimes called structural sufficiency, is a council or engineer's document about the structure and is not a statutory WA form. The Building Act's term for it is a technical certificate. The certificate of design compliance is the building surveyor's certificate that the plans comply.

### Do I need a permit to remove a wall?

Removing or altering an element that contributes to the support of another element is not exempt from a building permit. Whether your wall does that depends on the house, so an independent structural engineer needs to look at it. Nobody should tell you a wall can come out without seeing the building.
