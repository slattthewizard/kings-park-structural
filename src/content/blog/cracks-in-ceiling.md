## What cracks in the ceiling are usually telling you

A ceiling is a thin skin hung under the roof structure or the floor above. Plasterboard sheets are taped and set at the joints, and a cornice runs around the edge where ceiling meets wall. Those joints and edges are the weak points, so that's where a house that is moving, drying out or getting wet shows it first.

The CSIRO's homeowner guide to footings and foundation maintenance makes a point that applies here: symptoms "do not simply reflect the original cause". A crack tells you something has moved. It doesn't tell you what. Two ceilings with the same hairline can have different stories, one a bit of seasonal shrinkage and the other a beam that has deflected.

A few things narrow it down before anyone visits:

- **Where it runs.** A crack that follows a plasterboard joint or the line of a taped seam is a different thing from one that cuts across a sheet.
- **Whether it carries on down a wall.** A crack that starts in the ceiling and continues down the wall is telling you more than a ceiling crack on its own.
- **Whether it's changing.** A crack that was the same in March and September is easier to live with than one you can feel getting wider.
- **What else has moved.** Doors and windows that started sticking, gaps around skirting, or cracks in brickwork outside all point at the building rather than just the lining.

## Cornice gaps, joint lines and sagging: what each one looks like

Not every mark on a ceiling means the same thing. This table is a starting point for reading what you see. It isn't a diagnosis, and it doesn't replace someone looking at the house.

| What you see | Common causes | Worth watching or worth a call |
|---|---|---|
| Fine line along a taped joint between sheets | Plasterboard joint movement, temperature and moisture changes | Watch it. Photograph and date it. |
| Gap opening between cornice and wall, often at corners | Seasonal shrinkage and swelling of the ground under the house, roof timbers moving, old adhesive failing | Watch, especially if doors also stick. Call if it's widening. |
| Crack running across a sheet, or from ceiling down a wall | Movement in the wall, the floor above or the roof structure | Worth a call if it grows or lines up with wall cracks. |
| Crack along the line where a wall used to stand | Load path changed after wall removal, beam deflecting, or work done without engineering | Worth a call, particularly if the work had no engineer. |
| Brown stain with a soft, bulging or sagging section | A leaking roof, pipe or air-conditioning line | Treat as a water problem first. Get a roofer or plumber, not an engineer. |
| Ceiling dipping across a whole room, or visibly wavy | Roof or ceiling structure under load, moisture, or fixings failing | Worth a call. See [sagging roof truss problems](/blog/sagging-roof-truss-problems/). |

The CSIRO notes that in clay soils, seasonal swelling and shrinkage make doors bind and cornices crack. That's a real pattern, and it's why a cornice gap on its own can be a seasonal thing. It's also why we suggest checking the doors. A cornice gap plus a sticking door on the same side of the house is more useful information than either one alone.

## Three ceiling scenarios, anonymised

These are illustrations of the kind of question owners bring us, not case studies. They're anonymised and don't describe a particular job.

**A Kalamunda home whose back doors started sticking after a wet winter.** Say the owners notice the doors bind in July, then a gap opens between the cornice and the wall in the family room. The Perth Hills sit on ground where clay is well documented: the Guildford Formation is described as brown silty and slightly sandy clay across much of the eastern Perth region, and a Perth geotechnical firm notes clay sites "particularly in the Perth Hills". That doesn't tell you what soil is under this particular house, and no source assigns a site class to a named suburb. What it does mean is that seasonal movement is a reasonable thing to rule in or out, alongside a leaking pipe, downpipes discharging next to the footings, or an overwatered garden bed. CSIRO lists all of those as common causes of saturation. An engineer can look at the pattern across the house rather than one cornice.

**A 1970s double-brick home in Duncraig where the kitchen wall came out years ago.** Say a previous owner opened up the kitchen and a crack now runs along the ceiling where the wall used to stand. In a double-brick house, CSIRO notes that some internal walls usually carry upper floors, ceilings and roof. Taking out a wall that contributed to supporting something else is not exempt work under WA's Building Regulations 2012, so it needed a permit, and the beam that replaced the wall needed to be designed. If nobody can show you drawings or a technical certificate, that's worth finding out before you sell or add anything else. Our guide to [removing a load-bearing wall in Perth](/blog/removing-a-load-bearing-wall-perth/) covers what the process should have looked like.

**A hallway ceiling in a Bibra Lake home with a brown stain and a soft patch.** Say the plasterboard has dropped a few millimetres and feels damp. This one starts as a water problem, not a structural one. Find the source with a roofer or plumber first, because wet plasterboard can fail and come down. If the leak has been running a long time, the framing above may need a look too, and that's the point an engineer becomes useful. It's also worth checking your policy: many home policies exclude gradual leakage over time where the owner should have known, so check your own policy's wording.

## When cracks in ceiling and walls together point to something structural

A crack in the ceiling alone is often a finishing issue. It gets more interesting when it lines up with other signs. These are the combinations that are worth a proper look:

- A ceiling crack that continues into a wall crack, especially one that widens as it goes down.
- Stepped cracks in the brickwork outside, or cracks above windows and doors.
- Doors and windows that stick or no longer latch, on the same side of the house as the ceiling crack.
- A visible dip or bow in the ceiling, or an uneven roof line from the street.
- Cracking that appeared soon after nearby building work, a wall removal or a new extension.

For the wall cracks, the CSIRO damage categories give you a way to size what you're seeing. They come from AS 2870-2011 and stop at category 4:

- Category 1, very slight: cracks under 1 mm, fine and not needing repair.
- Category 2, slight: under 5 mm, noticeable, easily filled, doors and windows may stick slightly.
- Category 3, moderate: 5 to 15 mm, or several cracks of 3 mm or more in one group. Doors and windows stick and pipes can fracture.
- Category 4, severe: 15 to 25 mm. Extensive repair, frames distort, walls may lean or bulge.

Those widths describe cracks in walls. There's no equivalent published scale for ceiling plasterboard, so don't read a ceiling gap against them as though they were made for it. They're most useful for the wall cracks that go with a ceiling crack. CSIRO also says that most cracking in masonry walls is a cosmetic nuisance only, but monitoring "should always be treated seriously". Both statements are true at once, which is why measuring beats guessing. Our guide to [cracks in walls and when to worry](/blog/cracks-in-walls-when-to-worry/) walks through how to do it.

## What to do before you call anyone

Most of the useful work costs nothing and makes any later visit quicker.

1. **Photograph it with a ruler in frame.** Include the date. Take a wide shot of the room too, so the location is clear.
2. **Mark and date the ends.** A pencil line at each end of the crack and a date lets you see whether it grows.
3. **Note the season.** A gap that opens in summer and narrows in winter says something different from one that only ever widens.
4. **Check the roof space only if it's safe.** Ceiling joists don't take an adult's weight. Don't step off the walkway. If you can't get in without standing on plasterboard, skip it.
5. **Look for water.** Stains, drips, damp insulation or a musty smell. Deal with leaks first, since they change everything else.
6. **Note what changed.** A wall taken out, a new roof, a rerouted downpipe, a tree that grew, and any drawings or paperwork you hold.

If a section of ceiling is sagging, bulging or wet, keep people out from under it and get it made safe before anything else. That's not a job to wait on.

## What an engineer looks at, and what it costs in Perth

An independent structural engineer looking at a ceiling crack isn't looking at the ceiling. They're looking for what moved: the walls, the roof structure, the floor above, the footings, and any beam that carries the load. Firms that publish descriptions of a structural inspection describe checking walls and roof structure, load-bearing floors and ceilings, slab and footings, and signs of cracking, sagging or shifting. One Perth firm describes a visual assessment of accessible areas. If you do not need an engineer, a good one will say so.

One thing to get straight: a lot of what's sold as a "structural inspection" in Perth is a pre-purchase building inspection done by a building inspector, not an engineer's report. Both have a place, and they aren't the same. Our comparison of [a structural inspection versus a building inspection](/blog/structural-inspection-vs-building-inspection/) explains the difference.

On price, the honest position is that no Perth engineering firm we checked publishes a fee for a crack report. Engineers quote each job, and the price depends on the house, the structure and what you need it for. The Perth dollar figures that are published belong to building inspections: one firm states a building inspection report on an average Perth home is approximately $495 including GST, another starts at $440 including GST, and a pre-purchase structural and timber pest inspection is listed from $550 (GST not stated). Those are building-inspection products, not engineering fees. For an engineer's price, ask for a written quote after sending photos.

WA's registration rules are also worth knowing. Structural engineer registration began on 1 July 2024. A transition period runs until 1 July 2027, after which structural engineering work must be done by a registered building engineer. You can check status on the Building Engineer Register kept by Building and Energy (DEMIRS). The [WA Government's building engineering registration guide](https://www.wa.gov.au/government/multi-step-guides/building-engineering-registration) explains the scheme.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are cracks in the ceiling serious?

Often they aren't, but you can't tell from a photo. A fine line along a plasterboard joint is common and usually gets watched rather than fixed. A crack that widens, continues down a wall, or shows up alongside sticking doors or a sagging section is worth an engineer's opinion.

### What causes a cornice gap?

Common causes include seasonal movement of the ground under the house, roof timbers shifting, failed adhesive and plasterboard joint movement. CSIRO notes that in clay soils, seasonal swelling and shrinkage make cornices crack and doors bind. The cause depends on your house and site, so check the doors and outside walls too.

### Is a sagging ceiling dangerous?

It can be. If it's wet, bulging or dropping, keep people out from under it and get it made safe. A sag caused by a leak is a water problem first. A sag across a whole room, or one linked to the roof structure, needs a qualified person on site.

### Do I need an engineer or a building inspector?

It depends on the question. A building inspector reports on the general condition of a house, often for a purchase. An independent structural engineer assesses what moved and why, and designs fixes to the structure. If the worry is cracking, sagging or a removed wall, an engineer is the person to ask.
