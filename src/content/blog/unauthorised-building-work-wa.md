The patio was there when you bought the house. Or the previous owner knocked out the wall between the kitchen and the living room, and nobody can find the paperwork. Now the council, a buyer's solicitor or a valuer has asked for approval, and you have found out that there is none. This guide explains how Western Australia deals with work that was built without a permit, what the building approval certificate is, where a structural engineer fits, and what the government charges.

## What counts as unauthorised

Unauthorised work is building work that needed a building permit and did not get one. It is not the same as work that was exempt. Schedule 4 of the Building Regulations 2012 lists work that does not need a permit, and some common items sit inside it and some do not.

- A freestanding Class 10a building such as a small shed is exempt only if its floor area does not exceed 10 square metres, it is no more than 2.4 m high, and it is not in wind region C or D.
- A pergola associated with a house is exempt only within set limits, and the regulation defines a pergola as a structure that does not have a roof. A roofed patio is therefore not a pergola under that item.
- A retaining wall is exempt only if it retains no more than 0.5 m and is not associated with other building work.
- Renovation or alteration is exempt only if it does not affect structural soundness and does not include a change in floor area or height, underpinning or replacement of footings, or removal or alteration of an element contributing to the support of any other element.

That last item is why a removed internal wall often turns out to be unauthorised: if the wall was carrying load, taking it out falls outside the exemption. Our guide to [removing a load-bearing wall in Perth](/blog/removing-a-load-bearing-wall-perth/) covers that side.

Whether your particular structure was exempt is a question for your council or a building surveyor. If you are guessing, assume the answer has to be checked.

## The building approval certificate

When work has already been built without a permit, owners regularise it with a building approval certificate, using form BA13. The local government is the permit authority for houses, and it assesses the application. The City of Canning, for example, uses form BA13 for unauthorised Class 1 or Class 10 work. Other councils run their own versions of the process, and the steps and documents they ask for can differ, so start with your own council's building page.

One Perth firm describes the usual sequence like this:

1. Contact the council and check whether planning approval is also needed.
2. Lodge the BA13 application.
3. Have a registered building surveyor issue a BA18, the Certificate of Building Compliance.
4. Have a structural engineer inspect the work and certify it.

That is one firm's description of the path, not a rule. Councils vary on what they want, and the order can differ. Ask your council how long its own assessment takes, because no timeframe for this process is guaranteed.

## Where the structural engineer fits

The building surveyor decides whether the work complies. The engineer's job is narrower and more physical: to inspect what was built and say whether it is structurally adequate, and to put that in writing in a form the surveyor and council can use.

For a patio or carport, that usually means looking at the posts, the footings, the connections, the roof framing and how the structure is tied down. For a removed wall, it means working out what the wall was holding up, what is holding it up now, and whether the beam, posts and footings are adequate.

Councils use their own names for the engineer's document. The City of Perth, for instance, uses a form titled "Certificate of Structural Sufficiency", where the signatory states the AS 2870 site class and the design wind speed and lists the documents covered. WA has no statutory form called a "certificate of structural adequacy", so do not be thrown if your council uses another title. The page on [certificate of structural adequacy in WA](/blog/certificate-of-structural-adequacy-wa/) explains the naming.

Sometimes the inspection finds that the work is not adequate. A post may be undersized, a footing may be missing, or a beam may be too light. In that case the engineer designs a fix, a builder carries it out, and the engineer returns to confirm it. That is the real reason to budget time and money as a range rather than a single figure.

The engineer cannot see behind finished surfaces. Footings under a concrete slab, steel inside a boxed beam and fixings behind lining may need to be exposed before they can be judged. Expect that to be discussed.

## What it costs

There are two kinds of cost: government charges, and the engineer and builder.

**Government charges.** These are set by regulation. The fees below are from Building Regulations 2012 Schedule 2 and the wa.gov.au building approvals page, which was last updated on 4 August 2026. They change, so confirm the current figures with your council.

| Item | Fee |
|---|---|
| Building approval certificate, unauthorised work | 0.38% of estimated value, minimum $121.00 |
| Building Services Levy on that certificate, work over $45,000 | 0.274% of value |
| Building Services Levy on that certificate, work $45,000 or less | $123.30 |

The estimated value includes GST. As an illustration only, not a quote: on $30,000 of work, 0.38% would be $114, so the $121.00 minimum applies, and the levy would be $123.30. On $60,000 of work, 0.38% would be $228, and the 0.274% levy would be $164.40.

Older council fee sheets can show out-of-date minimums, so read the date on any fee schedule before relying on it.

**Engineer and builder.** No Perth engineering firm we checked publishes a fee for inspecting and certifying unauthorised work. Engineers quote each job; the price depends on the house, the structure and what the council needs. Any rectification work is a separate builder's quote. [Structural engineer cost in Perth](/structural-engineer-cost-perth/) sets out what is and is not published.

## Buying or selling a home with unapproved work

Unapproved work turns up most often at the point of sale. A buyer's inspection, a conveyancer's search of council records or a lender's valuer notices a patio or carport that is not on the plans. A removed wall shows up when the floor plan on file does not match the house.

If you are buying, the practical questions are who holds the problem after settlement, what it would take to regularise, and whether the structure is sound. A building inspector's pre-purchase report is not the same as an engineer's, and it is not a substitute for one. If the report flags a structure, ask whether an engineer should look at it. [Structural inspection vs building inspection](/blog/structural-inspection-vs-building-inspection/) explains the difference.

If you are selling, getting the structure looked at before listing gives you facts rather than surprises. It also lets you decide whether to regularise the work, or to disclose it and let the buyer decide. Your conveyancer or solicitor is the person to ask about contract terms, and we cannot advise on them.

Neither side should assume that an old structure is safe because it has stood for years, or unsafe because it has no paperwork. The only way to know is to have it looked at.

## Who is allowed to do what

Structural engineering in Western Australia is being brought under registration. Registration of building engineers began on 1 July 2024, and after a transition period ending 1 July 2027, structural building engineering work must be done by a registered building engineer. Ask any engineer who quotes you what their registration status is and check it on the [building engineering registration page on wa.gov.au](https://www.wa.gov.au/government/multi-step-guides/building-engineering-registration).

Engineers assess and design. Registered building surveyors issue the BA18. Builders carry out the work. Kings Park Structural Co does none of these, and cannot promise that any council will approve any structure.

## A sensible order of steps

1. **Find out what you have.** Ask the council what is on record, and photograph what was built.
2. **Check whether the work was exempt.** Compare it to the Schedule 4 conditions, or ask a surveyor.
3. **Ask your council for its BA13 process,** including what documents it wants.
4. **Get an engineer to inspect,** and ask what the inspection and certificate will cover before you agree.
5. **Price any fix separately,** using the engineer's scope for every builder quote.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a building approval certificate in WA?

It is the approval used to regularise building work that was built without a permit. Owners apply to their local government on form BA13, and the council assesses the application. A registered building surveyor issues a BA18, the Certificate of Building Compliance, and a structural engineer's inspection and certification is part of the usual path.

### How much does it cost to get unauthorised work approved?

The government fee is 0.38% of the estimated value of the work, with a minimum of $121.00, plus a Building Services Levy of 0.274% if the work is over $45,000 or $123.30 if it is $45,000 or less. Engineer and builder costs are quoted per job, and no Perth firm we checked publishes an engineer's fee.

### Can I sell a house with an unapproved patio or carport?

Nothing in the sources we read says you cannot. What it does is create a question for the buyer, their lender and their conveyancer. Ask your solicitor or conveyancer how it affects your contract, and consider getting the structure looked at before you list.

### Will the council refuse to approve the work?

We cannot predict that. The council assesses each application, and the engineer's inspection may show that the structure needs changes first. Ask your council what it requires before you spend money on drawings or inspections.
