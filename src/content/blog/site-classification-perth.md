A council form, an engineer's drawing or a slab quote asks for your "site class", and you have no idea what it is. The short version is that it is a label for how much the ground under your house is likely to move, and it sets the starting point for footing design. This guide explains the classes in AS 2870, what we know about Perth's ground, why a certificate asks for one, and why nobody should hand you a class based on your suburb.

## What a site class is

AS 2870 is the Australian Standard for residential slabs and footings. It sorts building sites into classes by how reactive the soil is, meaning how much it swells when wet and shrinks when dry. The standard itself is paywalled and we have not reproduced it here. The National Construction Code reproduces the definitions, and CSIRO's foundation maintenance guide reproduces the 2011 table.

The class is not a grade for a house or for a suburb. It is a description of one site, and it comes from looking at the ground on that site.

## The classes

| Class | What it describes | Characteristic surface movement (ys) |
|---|---|---|
| A | Most sand and rock sites, with little or no ground movement from moisture changes | Not applicable |
| S | Slightly reactive clay sites | 0 to 20 mm |
| M | Moderately reactive clay or silt sites | 20 to 40 mm |
| H1 | Highly reactive clay sites, high movement | 40 to 60 mm |
| H2 | Highly reactive clay sites, very high movement | 60 to 75 mm |
| E | Extremely reactive sites | Above 75 mm |
| P | Problem sites, see below | Not applicable |

The ys figures come from a secondary online calculator page, not from AS 2870 itself, so treat them as a guide to the scale rather than a citation of the standard. "ys" is the characteristic surface movement: how far the ground surface is expected to rise and fall between the wettest and driest conditions. Check the standard, or ask an engineer, for the authoritative values.

Class A has no ys range because the soil does not move meaningfully with moisture. That matters for cost and complexity. As the class climbs from S toward E, footings have to be designed to cope with more movement, and that generally means more steel, deeper or stiffer footings, and more attention to drainage and gardens.

### Class P

Class P is different from the others. It is not about how reactive the soil is. The NCC describes it as covering soft soils, loose sands, landslip, collapsing soils, soils subject to erosion, abnormal moisture conditions, and sites that cannot otherwise be classified. What a P site needs depends on the specific problem, and an engineer is the person to say.

## What we can say about Perth's ground

Geoscience Australia's report on natural hazard risk in Perth describes the main geology. We use only its geology here. The report's own "site classes" are earthquake classes, not AS 2870 classes.

- **Bassendean Sand** is present over much of the central Perth region. It is pale grey to white quartz sand, up to about 80 m thick, with a cemented layer called coffee rock near the water table.
- **Tamala Limestone** extends along the coastal strip. It is calcareous dune rock with numerous solution channels and cavities.
- **Spearwood Dune System** is slightly calcareous sand over Tamala Limestone, with the Quindalup dunes flanking the ocean.
- **Guildford Formation** is predominantly brown silty and slightly sandy clay, up to 35 m thick, and outcrops over much of the eastern Perth region. Its type area is the Swan Valley.
- **Pinjarra Plain** is predominantly clayey alluvium, about 5 km wide, and relevant to the Peel area.
- **Foothills** are colluvial slopes at the foot of the Darling Scarp.

A Perth geotechnical firm says that the most common soil type in the metropolitan area is sand, normally Class A, that soft or loose sand sites are Class P, and that there are clay sites, particularly in the Perth Hills. That is one firm's summary, but it matches the geology above: sand across the middle and coast, clay to the east and in the foothills.

## Why no suburb has a class

We do not list suburbs by class. Suburb-by-suburb soil mapping that we could rely on is not something we found, and for good reason. The ground changes within a street. A block on the edge of a dune system can sit on sand while a neighbour a few streets away has clay lenses. Fill, old creek lines, removed trees and earlier building work all change the answer.

The class is decided for a site by someone who has looked at that site, usually from soil testing, and written it down. Anyone who says your suburb is "all Class A" is guessing. That guess can be expensive if it is wrong, because the footing design follows the class.

## Why councils and certificates ask for it

A site class is a design input, so documents that rely on a design ask for it. The City of Perth's form F0162, titled "Certificate of Structural Sufficiency", is a clear example. The signatory states the site class under AS 2870 and the design gust wind speed, lists the documents the certificate covers, and confirms they are a Professional Engineer as defined in the National Construction Code.

The wording of that form belongs to that council. Other councils use their own forms and titles, and WA has no statutory form called a "certificate of structural adequacy". [Our guide to the certificate of structural adequacy in WA](/blog/certificate-of-structural-adequacy-wa/) explains the naming.

Where you tend to meet a site class in practice:

- an engineer's drawings for a slab, patio footing or extension;
- a building permit application, where a surveyor needs to see what the footings were designed for;
- a council certificate for work that has already been built;
- a builder's quote, which assumes a class and may change if the real one differs.

## How a class gets decided

The class comes from information about the soil on your block. For a house on a flat, well-drained sand site, a geotechnical report or an engineer's judgement may settle on Class A. For a clay site, the work is more involved, and the class depends on how reactive the clay is, how deep it goes and the local conditions, such as trees and drainage. Soil testing and classification reports are a separate business, usually carried out by geotechnical firms. ## What a class does not tell you

A site class is about movement of the soil because of moisture. It does not tell you whether a crack in your wall is serious, whether a leak is eroding sand under a footing, or whether a tree is drying a clay site. CSIRO's guidance lists leaking pipes, downpipes that are not connected to stormwater, overwatering and tree roots as common causes of movement, and those happen on any class. On sand, CSIRO notes that sandy soil is particularly susceptible to being washed away, which is why a Class A label is not a guarantee against problems.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the most common site class in Perth?

A Perth geotechnical firm says sand is the most common soil type in the metropolitan area and is normally Class A. The same firm says soft or loose sand sites are Class P and that clay sites occur, particularly in the Perth Hills. That describes the region, not your block, which needs its own classification.

### What is the difference between Class A and Class P?

Class A covers most sand and rock sites with little or no movement from moisture changes. Class P covers problem sites such as soft soils, loose sands, landslip, collapsing or eroding soils, abnormal moisture conditions, and sites that cannot otherwise be classified. P needs a specific design from an engineer.

### Can I find out my site class from my suburb?

No. The ground varies within a suburb and often within a street, and we found no suburb-level mapping to rely on. The class is set for a specific site, usually from soil testing and an engineer's or geotechnical firm's assessment.

### Who decides the site class for a building permit?

The class appears in the engineering documents that support the application, for example a certificate that states the site class under AS 2870. Your building surveyor and council decide what they need. Ask them what form and evidence they accept before you pay for testing or drawings.
