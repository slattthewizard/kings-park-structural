## What footing design actually decides

A footing is the part of the structure that spreads the building's weight onto the ground. Footing design is the engineer's job of working out how wide, how deep and how reinforced that footing needs to be, so the building sits still or at least moves within limits it can tolerate.

The answer is never a standard one. It depends on three things.

- **The ground.** Sand, limestone, clay and fill all behave differently, and one street can hold more than one of them.
- **The loads.** A lightweight patio roof puts very little on the ground. A double-brick extension with a tiled roof puts a lot. A second storey adds more again.
- **The existing house.** The new footings have to work alongside old ones without pulling the join apart.

Perth engineering firms describe slab and footing design for patios and sheds as "based on soil conditions", along with wind calculations to AS/NZS 1170.2 and a certificate for the council application. That phrase is worth holding onto. If a drawing does not say what ground it assumed, ask why.

## Why the ground comes first: site classes under AS 2870

Residential slabs and footings in Australia are classified against AS 2870, which sorts sites by how much the ground moves with moisture. The site class is the starting point for a footing design, and the City of Perth's own structural certificate form asks the signing engineer to state it.

| Site class | What it means | Characteristic surface movement |
|---|---|---|
| A | Most sand and rock sites, little or no movement from moisture changes | Little or none |
| S | Slightly reactive clay | 0 to 20 mm |
| M | Moderately reactive clay or silt | 20 to 40 mm |
| H1 | Highly reactive clay | 40 to 60 mm |
| H2 | Highly reactive clay, very high movement | 60 to 75 mm |
| E | Extremely reactive | Above 75 mm |
| P | Soft soils, loose sands, landslip, collapsing soils, erosion, abnormal moisture, or a site that cannot otherwise be classified | Assessed case by case |

The movement figures come from a secondary calculator page, not from AS 2870 itself, which is paywalled. Treat them as a guide to scale. The engineer works from the standard.

A Perth geotechnical firm says the most common soil type across the metropolitan area is sand, normally Class A, with soft or loose sand sites falling into Class P and clay sites turning up "particularly in the Perth Hills". Geoscience Australia describes clayey ground across much of the eastern Perth region (the Guildford Formation, brown silty and slightly sandy clay) and along the Pinjarra Plain in Peel. Nobody publishes a class for each suburb, so we won't guess one for yours. A site class comes from investigating your block.

## Three anonymised scenarios

These are composites of the kinds of jobs that come up, not case studies of real clients.

**A Bibra Lake family adding a second storey to a 1980s brick-and-tile.** The first question is not the new upper floor. It is whether the existing walls and footings can carry the extra load at all. No Perth source we could find sets out a standard scope for that check, so the engineer decides what needs to be investigated, and that is one reason the quote depends on the house. If the answer is that existing footings need strengthening, the work moves into replacement or underpinning territory, which is never exempt from a building permit.

**A Perth Hills home with a new rear extension, where clay is reported in the area.** Clay near the surface swells in winter and shrinks in summer, and the extension will move on its own schedule while the old house does something slightly different. The classic sign of trouble is a crack at the join between old and new, or doors that start to bind after a wet winter. The engineer's job is to design footings and a joint detail that let the two parts of the building live together. Trees close to the new footings matter here, because roots can lift a footing or dry the clay and shrink it. Our guide on [tree roots cracking a house](/blog/tree-roots-cracking-house/) covers what to look for.

**A Baldivis family enclosing an alfresco.** An open patio and a lived-in room are different structures. Once walls, doors and a proper floor go in, the loads and the tolerance for movement change, and the engineer may want a different footing from the one the patio would have had. Owners are often surprised that the original concrete pour is not necessarily what the new design assumes.

## Why an extension is not exempt from a permit

In Western Australia, the Building Regulations 2012 list what does not need a building permit. A renovation or alteration is exempt only if it does not affect structural soundness and does not include an increase or decrease in floor area or height, underpinning or replacement of footings, or removal or alteration of an element that contributes to the support of another.

An extension changes floor area. A second storey changes height. Both fall outside the exemption, so a permit is needed, and the application will need an engineer's details. City of Rockingham's uncertified-application checklist, for example, calls for a copy of the "structural engineers details", designed and certified by a Practising Structural Engineer.

Alfrescos sit in a grey area that owners often get wrong. A freestanding Class 10a building can be exempt only if its floor area is not more than 10 m2, it is no higher than 2.4 m, and it is not in wind region C or D. A pergola associated with a house can be exempt up to 20 m2 and 2.4 m, but the regulations define a pergola as a structure that does not have a roof, apart from an open-weave covering. A roofed alfresco is therefore not a pergola under that item. Before you assume yours is exempt, ask your local council. ## What the permit charges look like

Footings are only part of a permit application, so it helps to know what the government side costs. These figures come from the Building Regulations 2012, Schedule 2, and the wa.gov.au building approvals page.

| Charge | Amount |
|---|---|
| Certified building permit, Class 1 or Class 10 | 0.19% of estimated value, not less than $121.00 |
| Uncertified building permit | 0.32% of estimated value, not less than $121.00 |
| Building Services Levy on a permit | 0.137% of value if over $45,000, or $61.65 if $45,000 or less |
| Construction Training Fund levy | 0.2% of the value of the work including GST, only where the value is more than $100,000 |

The value of the work is stated including GST. To show how it works, take a hypothetical $80,000 extension, a number chosen only for the arithmetic. A certified permit at 0.19% comes to $152, and the Building Services Levy at 0.137% is about $110. The Construction Training Fund levy would not apply because the job is under $100,000.

Some council fee sheets still show older figures, such as a $110 minimum and a $20,000 levy threshold. The regulations now say $121.00 and $100,000, so check the current wa.gov.au page rather than an old PDF. The regulations also set decision periods of 10 business days for certified applications and 25 for uncertified ones, but those clocks stop when the authority asks for more information, so they say little about how long your job takes overall.

## What we can and cannot tell you about the engineer's fee

No Perth engineering firm we checked publishes a price for footing and slab design, for an extension, or for a second-storey assessment. That is the honest state of the market, and it is why you will see wildly different numbers on lead-generation sites. Engineers quote each job; the price depends on the house, the structure and what the council needs.

The best you can do is send the engineer good information before the quote. A photo of the site, the plans if you have them, the age of the house, and any cracking or sticking doors near the area you are building will all help. If an interstate or national average is quoted at you, remember it is not a Perth price, and it knows nothing about your ground.

## Getting the design and the build in the right order

The sequence matters more than most owners realise.

1. **Find out what the council needs.** Ask which documents your application requires, and whether it goes through the certified or uncertified pathway.
2. **Involve the engineer before the slab.** Footings are the one part of a building you cannot cheaply change once the concrete is poured.
3. **Have the site class established.** The engineer states it and designs to it.
4. **Tell the engineer the existing house's history.** Old cracks, leaking pipes, downpipes not connected to stormwater and overwatered gardens are all documented causes of ground movement. CSIRO notes that even a minuscule leak can be enough to saturate a clay foundation.
5. **Check the engineer's status.** From 1 July 2027, structural engineering in WA must be done by a registered building engineer, and you can check the DEMIRS Building Engineer Register. Some pages still say 1 July 2026, but that date has been superseded.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is footing design and who does it?
Footing design is the calculation and detailing of the footings and slab that carry a building's weight into the ground. In Western Australia, structural engineering must be done by a registered building engineer from 1 July 2027, and you can check the DEMIRS register. We are a referral service and do not do this work ourselves.

### Do I need new footing design for an alfresco?
It depends on the structure. A roofed alfresco is not a pergola under the exemption for pergolas, and an attached structure is not a freestanding building. Ask your council what it needs, and the engineer will tell you what the design and certificate should cover.

### How much does footing design cost in Perth?
No Perth engineering firm we checked publishes a price for footing or slab design. Engineers quote each job, and the price depends on the house, the structure and what the council needs. Government charges are separate and public, and the permit fee minimum is currently $121.00.

### Can my existing footings carry a second storey?
That is a question for an engineer to answer after seeing your plans and the house. A second storey adds weight, so the existing walls and footings have to be checked, and replacing or underpinning footings takes the work outside the permit exemption.
