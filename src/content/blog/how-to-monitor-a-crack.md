A crack appears in a wall and the first question is whether it is getting worse. One look cannot answer that. Two readings a few weeks apart can. Monitoring is simple, cheap and worth doing properly, because the record you build is also what an engineer will want to see if you need one. This guide sets out a method, the CSIRO damage categories to compare your readings against, and the signs that mean you should stop monitoring and get a professional.

## Why monitor at all

CSIRO's footing guidance says most cracking found in masonry walls is a cosmetic nuisance only. It also says monitoring should always be treated seriously. Most cracks are minor, but the ones that are not minor look the same as the ones that are until they change.

Monitoring tells you three things a single look cannot: whether the crack is moving, whether it moves with the seasons, and whether new cracks are appearing. A crack that has not changed in a year tells a different story from one that has doubled in a month.

## What you need

- A phone camera
- A tape measure or ruler you can leave in the photo
- A pencil
- A crack-width card or a set of feeler gauges (hardware and building supply shops sell both)
- A notebook, or a note on your phone, with a date for every entry

That is the whole kit. Nothing here involves cutting, drilling or repairing the wall, and you should not fill or paint over the crack while you are still monitoring it, because that destroys the record.

## Step 1: Photograph it, with a tape in the frame

Take a wide shot that shows where the crack sits in the room or on the wall, so anyone can find it later. Then take a close-up with a tape measure or ruler laid beside the crack. The scale in the frame is what turns a photo into a measurement.

Photograph from the same spot each time. Label each image with the date, or keep a folder with one sub-folder per crack. Number the cracks on a rough sketch of the house so you know which is which.

## Step 2: Mark the ends in pencil

Draw a short line across the crack at each end and write the date next to it. If the crack grows, it will run past the pencil mark, and you will see how far and when. Use pencil rather than pen or marker, so you are not adding anything permanent to the wall.

For a longer crack, you can add a mark at the midpoint as well. The marks show length change. They do not show width, which is the next step.

## Step 3: Measure the width

Hold a crack-width card against the widest part of the crack and find the printed line it matches. Feeler gauges work the same way: slide the thickest gauge that fits into the crack and note its thickness. Record the number in millimetres, with the date and the position, for example "kitchen window, left corner, top".

Measure at the same place each time. Mark that spot in pencil so you return to it. Measure twice and write down both readings if they differ, because the edges of a crack are rarely clean.

## Step 4: Record on a schedule, including by season

Take a set of readings about every two to four weeks, and always after notable weather such as a long dry spell or heavy rain. Do not rely on memory.

Seasons matter more than owners expect. On reactive clay, CSIRO notes that seasonal swelling and shrinkage cause dishing and doming, and doors bind and cornices crack as a result. A crack that opens in summer and narrows in winter, or the reverse, may be moving with the soil moisture. That is useful for an engineer to know. Keep recording through at least a full cycle of seasons where you can, and keep each reading dated so the pattern shows.

## Step 5: Note the doors, windows and other clues

A crack does not tell its whole story alone. In the same notebook, note:

- Doors or windows that stick or will not latch, and in which season
- New cracks, even hairline ones, anywhere in the house
- Cornices pulling away from walls or ceilings
- Gaps opening between walls and paths, or between walls and the ceiling
- Anything nearby that changed: a new garden bed, a tree, a leaking tap, a downpipe moved

Sticking doors belong in the record. In CSIRO's damage categories, doors and windows sticking is part of the picture from slight damage upward.

## Comparing your readings with the CSIRO categories

CSIRO's guide reproduces the damage categories from AS 2870-2011. They give approximate crack width limits and the typical damage that goes with them.

| Category | Approx. crack width | Typical damage |
|---|---|---|
| 0 Negligible | Under 0.1 mm | Hairline cracks |
| 1 Very slight | Under 1 mm | Fine cracks not needing repair |
| 2 Slight | Under 5 mm | Noticeable, easily filled; doors and windows stick slightly |
| 3 Moderate | 5 to 15 mm, or several cracks of 3 mm or more in one group | Some wall replacement possible; doors and windows stick; pipes can fracture |
| 4 Severe | 15 to 25 mm, also depends on number of cracks | Extensive repair; frames distort; walls lean or bulge |

Use this table to put a name to what you measure, not to decide a crack is fine. The width is only one input. The number of cracks, where they are and whether they are changing all count, and the categories are approximate. A crack in category 1 that is widening week by week deserves more attention than a stable crack in category 2.

## When to stop monitoring and get an engineer

Monitoring is for cracks that look stable. Some situations call for an engineer instead of more readings:

1. **The crack is widening.** Successive measurements keep going up, or the pencil mark has been overrun.
2. **It is category 3 or worse.** A crack of 5 mm or more, or several cracks of 3 mm or more in one group, sits at the moderate category and above.
3. **New cracks keep appearing.** Especially if they are in different parts of the house.
4. **Doors and windows are binding.** Particularly if it is getting worse, or happening in more than one room.
5. **The wall is leaning, bulging or sagging,** or a frame looks distorted.
6. **You are unsure.** If you cannot tell what you are looking at, an assessment costs less than a wrong guess.

CSIRO says diagnosis is complicated because the symptoms do not simply reflect the original cause. ## A few cautions

Do not fill or paint the crack until you know the cause. Repairing it hides the evidence and does nothing about what is moving. Do not assume a small crack is harmless because it is small, and do not assume a big one is a disaster. Neither we nor anyone else can promise a crack is harmless or a house is safe from photos alone. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How often should I measure a crack?

Every two to four weeks is a reasonable rhythm, plus a reading after any notable change in weather. Keep going through a full cycle of seasons where you can, because some cracks open and close with the soil moisture. Date every reading.

### Can I just fill the crack and be done?

Filling a crack before you know the cause hides the evidence and does not stop whatever is moving. It also ruins your record. Monitor first, and get an engineer's view if the crack is widening, new cracks appear or doors start to bind.

### What crack width is serious?

CSIRO's categories give approximate limits, with category 3 starting around 5 mm, or several cracks of 3 mm or more in one group. Width is only one factor, though. A narrow crack that keeps widening, or appears with sticking doors and new cracks, can matter more than a wide stable one.

### Do I need an engineer if the crack is not changing?

Not necessarily. CSIRO says most cracking in masonry walls is a cosmetic nuisance only, and a crack that stays stable through the seasons may need no action. If you are unsure, or you want a professional view before buying, selling or building, an engineer can assess it.
