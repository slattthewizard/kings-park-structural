# Sagging Roof or Broken Truss? Signs and What an Engineer Checks

You are standing in the street looking at your own house, and the ridge line is no longer straight. ## Signs of a sagging roof you can see from the ground

Start outside, in daylight, with the sun low so shadows show up any dips. Look along the ridge and the eaves.

The signs worth noting are these:

- A ridge line that dips or bows instead of running straight.
- Roof tiles that have shifted, gapped or ridden up over each other.
- A fascia or gutter line that has gone wavy, or a roof plane that looks dished between the walls.
- Gaps opening between the top of an internal wall and the ceiling above it.
- Ceiling cracks that run along a wall junction or follow the line of a truss.
- Internal doors that have started to bind for no obvious reason.

None of these proves a structural fault on its own. Tile roofs on older houses often show a gentle wave because timber settles and creeps over decades. What matters is whether the movement is new, growing, or paired with other signs. A photo from the same spot every month, with a string line in frame, is the cheapest monitoring tool you have.

Look inside too, if you can do it safely. Through the manhole you might see a bent truss chord, a split near a connection plate, or a loose brace. Do not step on the ceiling battens or insulation, because ceiling linings will not carry a person. If you are not comfortable up there, skip it and describe the ceiling cracks instead.

## What actually causes a roof to sag

Roof structures in Perth homes come in two broad types, and the difference changes what can go wrong.

Older homes, and some later custom builds, often have a cut-and-pitch roof. Rafters, ceiling joists and struts are cut and nailed on site, and the roof is often held up partly by internal walls through struts and underpurlins. Most houses built from the 1980s onward use prefabricated trusses, which span from one external wall to the other and are held together by metal plates at each joint.

The usual causes of a visible sag are:

- **Undersized or overloaded timber.** A rafter or ceiling joist that was marginal to begin with can creep under the weight of a heavy concrete tile roof over many years.
- **Failed connections.** Plates that have pulled out, nails that have withdrawn, or a joint that was never properly fixed.
- **Moisture and rot.** A slow leak, a failed flashing or a blocked gutter can soften timber at the wall plate or around a valley.
- **Timber pests.** A pest inspector finds them; an engineer assesses what the damage means for the structure.
- **Later alterations.** A truss chord cut for a hatch, a beam removed or a wall taken out below can put loads where the roof was never designed to carry them.
- **Foundation movement.** If a wall has moved, the roof it supports moves with it. CSIRO notes that diagnosis is complicated because symptoms do not simply reflect the original cause, so what looks like a roof problem is sometimes a footing problem, and the reverse.

That last point is why an engineer looks at the whole load path, not just the roof. Load comes down from the tiles into the rafters or trusses, into the wall plates, down the walls and into the footings. A fault anywhere along that path can show up as a sagging roof.

## Roof truss problems: what an engineer checks on site

If you ask an independent structural engineer to look at a roof, the visit typically works through several questions. Each engineer sets their own scope, and what is covered depends on the house.

**Which type of roof is it?** The first job is working out whether the roof is trussed or cut-and-pitch, and where the load actually goes. In a cut-and-pitch roof, internal walls, struts and underpurlins may be part of the support. That matters a great deal if you are also planning to remove a wall. Our guide to [how to tell if a wall is load-bearing](/blog/how-to-tell-if-a-wall-is-load-bearing/) explains why a wall under a roof is not automatically holding it up, and not automatically free either.

**Where is the movement, and is it still moving?** An engineer will usually measure the sag, look for signs of recent versus old movement,.

**What is the condition of the members and connections?** That means checking chords, webs, rafters, struts, plates and fixings for splits, rot, insect damage or pull-out. An engineer can flag suspected timber pest damage, but a pest inspector confirms it.

**What is on the roof?** Concrete tiles over a structure designed for lighter cladding is a classic overload. So is an added solar array with no engineering check.

**What is the wall and footing story?** If cracking runs through the brickwork below the sagging section, or doors and windows are sticking, the engineer will look at the walls and footings as part of the assessment. See our guide to [cracks in brick walls](/blog/cracks-in-brick-walls/) for how movement shows up there.

The outcome is usually a written finding: monitor it, design a repair, or investigate further. It is not a guarantee about the future.

## Three field scenarios

These are anonymised composites of the kind of enquiry Perth owners describe, not case studies.

**A 1970s cut-and-pitch home in Duncraig with a dipping ridge.** The owners notice the ridge sags between two points and that a ceiling crack has opened along one internal wall. In this kind of house the internal wall may carry struts up to an underpurlin. If they removed that wall for an open-plan kitchen, the roof could lose a support. The brief to the engineer covers both the existing sag and the proposed wall removal, and the answer may come with a beam or with a no. No Perth firm publishes a fee for this work; engineers quote each job, and the price depends on the house, the structure and what the council needs.

**A Kalamunda home on clay where the ceiling has developed hairline cracks and the back doors stick after a wet winter.** The owners assume the roof has failed. In the eastern suburbs and Perth Hills, Guildford Formation clay outcrops over much of the region, and seasonal swelling and shrinkage of reactive clay can move footings and walls, which then shows up in the ceiling. CSIRO's damage categories rank cracking by width: under 1 mm is very slight, under 5 mm slight, and 5 to 15 mm moderate. The engineer's job is to work out whether the roof is the cause or the messenger. No source assigns a soil class to a named suburb, so the site class for this particular block has to be confirmed rather than assumed.

**A Rockingham house with a stepped crack above a window, two streets from the beach.** Coastal salt air is hard on steel, and a WA building inspection firm describes coastal WA as the perfect rust storm for steel lintels, with rust expansion cracking the brickwork and mortar around them. If the crack sits near a lintel that carries part of the roof load, the engineer will want to see it. This one is a wall and lintel question first, and a roof question second. ## Repair, permits and what it costs

Engineers assess and design; registered builders build. We do not arrange building work.

Whether the work needs a building permit depends on what it is. Under the Building Regulations 2012, a renovation or alteration is exempt from a building permit only if it does not affect structural soundness and does not include changes such as underpinning or replacing footings, or removing or altering an element that helps support another element. Replacing or strengthening roof framing will usually touch structural soundness, so ask your local government or a building surveyor rather than assuming it is exempt. The engineer's drawings and any certificate typically support that application. Our guide to the [certificate of structural adequacy in WA](/blog/certificate-of-structural-adequacy-wa/) explains what these certificates are called.

The government charges are the firm figures in this area, so here they are, from Building Regulations 2012 Schedule 2 and the related state levies.

| Charge | Amount |
|---|---|
| Certified building permit (Class 1 or Class 10) | 0.19% of estimated value, not less than $121.00 |
| Uncertified building permit | 0.32% of estimated value, not less than $121.00 |
| Building approval certificate for unauthorised work | 0.38% of estimated value, not less than $121.00 |
| Building Services Levy, permit, work of $45,000 or less | $61.65 |
| Building Services Levy, permit, work over $45,000 | 0.137% of value |
| Construction Training Fund levy | 0.2% of value, only where work is over $100,000 |

Take a repair valued at $30,000. The certified permit rate would come to $57, so the $121.00 minimum applies, and the levy would be $61.65. Do not treat that as a quote: permit value is the estimated value of the work including GST, and your builder and surveyor will settle it.

What we cannot give you is the engineer's fee. No Perth engineering firm publishes a price for a roof or crack assessment, so anything you read online that quotes one is an estimate. The nearest published Perth figures are building-inspection products, for example a pre-purchase report from $440 including GST at one firm, or around $495 at another (GST treatment varies). Those are inspections by building inspectors, not engineer's reports, and a building inspector's report is not a substitute for an engineer's assessment of a sagging roof. ## When to act quickly and when to keep watching

Sudden movement, a crack that widens over days, a ceiling that has visibly dropped or split timber all call for a prompt look. If a ceiling looks close to failing, keep people out of that room and get it looked at rather than watching for another week.

For a slow, old sag with no other symptoms, the sensible approach is to record it. Measure, photograph and date it. If nothing changes across a season, that is information, and it makes the eventual engineer conversation quicker. Most sags are gradual, a few are not, and you cannot tell which from the driveway.

Do not add weight to a sagging roof while you decide.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a sagging roof always a structural emergency?

No. Many roofs show a gentle dip that has been there for decades, particularly older timber roofs under concrete tile. What matters is whether the movement is new, getting worse, or paired with cracks, sticking doors or a dropping ceiling. If you cannot tell, an independent structural engineer can assess it. Sudden change or a ceiling that has visibly lowered should be treated as urgent.

### Can I tell if a roof truss is broken from inside the roof space?

Sometimes. Look for a bent or split chord, a joint plate that has pulled away, or a brace that has come loose, but do not walk on the ceiling lining. Plasterboard will not carry a person. If you cannot get up there safely, photograph what you can and describe the ceiling cracks to the engineer.

### Does fixing a sagging roof need a building permit in WA?

Often, yes. A renovation is exempt only if it does not affect structural soundness and avoids things like removing or altering an element that supports another element. Strengthening roof framing usually touches structural soundness, so ask your local government or a building surveyor before you start. The engineer's drawings typically support the application.

### Will my home insurance cover a sagging roof?

That depends on your policy and the cause. Many home policies exclude gradual subsidence and ground movement, and some exclude structural failure or defects unless the owner could not reasonably have known of them. Check your own policy's exclusions, and read our guide to [subsidence insurance in WA](/blog/subsidence-insurance-wa/) before you lodge anything.
