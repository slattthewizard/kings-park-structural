# Load Bearing Wall in a Double-Brick Home: What Changes in Perth

You have a tape measure, a Pinterest board of open-plan kitchens and a quote from a builder who says the wall "should be fine to come out". Before anyone picks up a hammer, the question that decides your budget is whether that wall is a load bearing wall, and in a double-brick house the honest answer is often "possibly, and it needs checking".

This guide explains why double-brick homes behave differently, what the WA rules say about taking out a wall that holds something up, and what you can sensibly find out before you spend anything. ## Why load-bearing walls in double-brick homes are a different question

Perth builds in brick. An HIA-commissioned report from 2025, cited by ABC News on 27 June 2026, found Western Australia was the only state where more than half of detached houses were built of brick, at 68 per cent. That matters because a brick house does not always carry its weight the way a timber-framed one does.

CSIRO's guide to foundation maintenance and footing performance notes that in full-masonry (double-brick) houses, the inner leaf of the external walls and at least some of the internal walls usually carry the upper floors, ceilings and roof. In plain terms, a double-brick internal wall is more likely to be doing structural work than a plasterboard partition in a timber-frame house. That is our reading of the CSIRO wording, and CSIRO does not discuss wall removal as such.

"More likely" is not "definitely". Plenty of internal walls in brick homes carry nothing but their own weight. The difference comes down to how the roof and any upper floor are framed, where the ends of the timbers land, and whether earlier renovators changed things without telling anyone. A wall can also be carrying load in one section and not in another.

There is a second wrinkle. When a wall that carries load is cut out, that load has to go somewhere, usually into a beam and then down through posts to footings. The beam is the visible part of the job. The footing under each end of it is the part people forget.

## Clues that a wall might be a load bearing wall

You cannot confirm anything by looking, but you can gather better information for the engineer. Some pointers, none of them proof:

- **What sits above it.** A wall directly under another wall, or under the join of ceiling framing, is more suspicious than one with open roof space and nothing landing on it.
- **Where it sits in the plan.** Walls near the middle of the house, running the long way, are worth a closer look than short stub walls at the edge of a room.
- **Old renovations.** A doorway opened up years ago, or a bulkhead that follows the line of a former wall, can mean the load was already picked up by something you cannot see.

Our guide on [how to tell if a wall is load-bearing](/blog/how-to-tell-if-a-wall-is-load-bearing/) goes through these in more detail. Take photos of the wall, the ceiling above it from inside the roof space if it is safe to look, and any existing cracks. Photos and plans give an engineer something to quote from.

## Three Perth homes, three different answers

These are anonymised, typical situations, not client stories. They show how much the answer depends on the house.

### A 1970s double-brick home in Duncraig, kitchen wall

The owners want the wall between kitchen and dining room gone. It is double brick, about the same thickness as the external walls, and it lines up with a change in the ceiling. That combination is a reason to take the wall seriously, not proof that it carries load.

Removing or altering something that supports another element is not exempt building work under the Building Regulations 2012, so a building permit is needed. For a job valued at, say, $30,000 including GST (an illustration, not a quote), the permit charge at 0.19% for a certified application would be $57, but the regulation minimum is $121.00, so $121.00 applies. The Building Services Levy adds $61.65 for work valued at $45,000 or less. Those are government charges only. The engineer's fee, the beam, the propping and the builder are separate, and none of the Perth engineering firms we checked publishes a fee for beam design. They quote each job.

### A stepped crack above a window in a Rockingham house two streets from the beach

Stepped cracking through the mortar joints above a window is a classic pattern. Two explanations are worth an engineer's time. One is ground movement or erosion, where CSIRO describes step cracking in mortar and vertical cracks at openings. The other is a steel lintel that has rusted and swollen, which a WA building-inspection firm has described as a real risk on the coast because the expanding rust pushes the brickwork apart.

CSIRO's damage categories give a rough scale: a crack under 5 mm falls in category 2, "slight", which it describes as noticeable and easily filled, with doors and windows sticking slightly. CSIRO also says most cracking in masonry walls is a cosmetic nuisance, but that monitoring should always be treated seriously. If a crack sits above an opening in a wall you plan to alter, the crack is part of the brief. Our guide to [cracks in brick walls](/blog/cracks-in-brick-walls/) goes into brick cracking in more detail.

### A Perth Hills home on a clay lot where the back doors started sticking

The owners want to open up the living area, but since a wet winter the back doors bind and a cornice has cracked. Clay sites are reported particularly in the Perth Hills, and the Guildford Formation clay outcrops over much of the eastern Perth region, though no source assigns a site class to a particular suburb or street.

CSIRO lists seasonal swelling and shrinking of clay, leaking pipes, downpipes that dump water beside the footing, over-watering from automatic systems and tree roots as common causes of movement. Doors that bind and cornices that crack are among its listed symptoms. If the footings are moving, a new beam bearing on them is a bigger question. An engineer will want to know about the doors before quoting the wall, not after.

## The rules: permit, timeframes and who can sign it

WA treats this as building work with a paper trail. Some points that trip people up:

**A permit is needed.** A renovation is exempt only if it does not affect structural soundness and does not remove or alter an element contributing to the support of any other element. Taking out a wall that supports the roof or a floor fails that test. Footing work, including underpinning or replacing footings, also falls outside the exemption. The [wall removal engineering page](/wall-removal-engineering/) has the service overview.

**There are two application routes.** A certified application (BA1) includes a certificate of design compliance from a private building surveyor. An uncertified application (BA2) goes to the local government, whose surveyors assess it. Uncertified applications are available only for Class 1a and Class 10 buildings. The City of Rockingham lists a copy of structural engineer's details, designed and certified by a practising structural engineer, among the documents it wants for an uncertified application.

**Decision periods are set in regulation.** The statutory period is 10 business days for a certified application and 25 business days for an uncertified one, or longer if agreed in writing. The clock stops when the authority asks for more information. Those are decision periods, not a promise about the whole job, and they start once a complete application is in.

**The "certificate" has more than one name.** WA law speaks of a technical certificate. Councils use their own forms and wording, and terms such as certificate of structural adequacy or structural sufficiency are common in the trade. Read what your building surveyor or council actually asks for.

**Registration is changing.** Registration of structural building engineers began on 1 July 2024, with a transition period until 1 July 2027. After that date, structural engineering work must be done by a registered building engineer, and the DEMIRS Building Engineer Register is where you check. The [WA Government's building engineering registration guide](https://www.wa.gov.au/government/multi-step-guides/building-engineering-registration) explains the scheme. "Engineer" and "structural engineer" are not protected titles in WA, so ask for the registration details rather than trusting the label.

## Before you commit to anything

A few habits save money on any wall you suspect is carrying load:

1. **Do not start demolition to "have a look".** Once lining is off and a brick is out, the wall has changed.
2. **Collect what you have.** Original plans if you can find them, photos of the ceiling space, a list of past renovations and a note of any cracks or sticking doors.
3. **Ask what the beam sits on.** A [steel beam for wall removal](/blog/steel-beam-for-wall-removal/) is only as good as the posts and footings under it. Ask who is checking those, not just the beam size.
4. **Ask for the scope in writing.** Is it a look at the wall, a design, or a certificate for the permit? They are different jobs with different prices.
5. **Check the registration.** Look the engineer up on the DEMIRS register.

Cracks or movement are a different conversation, and the [structural inspections page](/structural-inspections/) is the place to start. An engineer's report is not the same as a pre-purchase building inspection, and one Perth firm says plainly that its reports do not include structural design or engineering certification.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is an internal wall in a double-brick house always load-bearing?

No. CSIRO says at least some internal walls in a full-masonry house usually carry upper floors, ceilings or roof, but plenty carry only their own weight. Only someone looking at the roof and floor framing, and ideally the plans, can tell you which yours is.

### Do I need a building permit to remove a load bearing wall in WA?

Generally yes. Work that removes or alters an element supporting another element falls outside the building permit exemption in the Building Regulations 2012. The permit application usually needs an engineer's details, and your local government or a private building surveyor decides it.
