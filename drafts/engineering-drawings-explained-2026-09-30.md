## What engineering drawings are and who uses them

A builder's floor plan shows where rooms and walls go. Engineering drawings show what holds the building up. They set out the structural parts of the job in enough detail for a builder to build from and for a building surveyor to check: which members go where, what size they are, what they sit on and how they connect.

That usually means a plan showing the structural changes, a section or elevation through the beam or roof, and detail sheets for the connections and footings. There will be notes as well, and the notes matter as much as the lines. They record the assumptions the engineer used, such as the soil classification and the design wind speed.

Three different people read the same set for three different reasons.

**Your builder** uses it to order steel or LVL beams, set out posts and pad footings, and plan the temporary propping. A Perth builder's own description of a wall removal package lists engineering design and certification, the building permit, beams, temporary propping, posts, pad footings and load transfer. The drawings are the instructions for most of that list.

**The building surveyor** checks the design against the building rules and decides whether to sign the certificate of design compliance. In our guide to the [certificate of design compliance in WA](/blog/certificate-of-design-compliance-wa/) we go through that step.

**The council** is the permit authority for houses in WA. It issues the permit, but it doesn't redesign anything. If the drawings are missing or vague, it asks for more, and that costs you time.

One point about titles. "Engineer" and "structural engineer" are not reserved titles in WA today. What is reserved are titles such as "Registered Building Engineer". From 1 July 2027, structural engineering work must be done by a registered building engineer, and you can check the Building Engineer Register kept by Building and Energy (DEMIRS). The [WA Government's building engineering registration page](https://www.wa.gov.au/government/multi-step-guides/building-engineering-registration) explains the scheme. A drawing with the word "engineer" on it tells you little. The signatory's name, qualifications and register details tell you more.

## What structural engineering drawings show on a Perth wall removal

Start with the case most owners ask about: a 1970s double-brick home in Duncraig where the owners want the kitchen wall gone to open up the living area.

The first question is not "what size beam". It's "is this wall carrying anything?" CSIRO notes that in full-masonry houses the inner leaf of the external walls and at least some internal walls usually carry upper floors, ceilings and roof. In a double-brick house, an internal wall can be doing real work. Whether this one is depends on the roof structure and framing above it, and that has to be checked in the house, not guessed from a photo.

Removing an element that helps support another element is also one of the things that takes a renovation outside the building permit exemption. So a load-bearing wall comes out with a permit.

If the engineer finds the wall is load-bearing and can be replaced with a beam, the drawings typically show:

- the beam's position, span, size and material (steel or LVL);
- the posts that carry each end and where they land;
- pad footings under those posts, since the load has to reach the ground;
- how the beam connects to the roof or floor above;
- notes on temporary propping while the wall is out.

If the engineer finds the wall isn't load-bearing, there may be no beam to draw, and the answer still settles the builder's "engineer to confirm" line. Our page on [wall removal engineering](/wall-removal-engineering/) covers the job in more detail, and the guide to [steel beams for wall removal](/blog/steel-beam-for-wall-removal/) explains what the beam options involve.

What does it cost? Engineers quote each job, and no Perth engineering firm publishes a fee for beam design. The price depends on the house, the structure and what the council needs. The permit charges are different, because they're set by regulation. Take an illustrative $30,000 renovation. A certified building permit is 0.19% of the estimated value, but not less than $121.00. On $30,000 the percentage comes to $57, so the $121.00 minimum applies. The Building Services Levy for work of $45,000 or less is $61.65. Those are government charges, not engineering fees, and the value is counted including GST.

## What a Perth council or building surveyor asks for

The Building Act 2011 gives you two routes to a permit, and the drawings travel a little differently on each.

On the **certified** route (form BA1), you engage a registered private building surveyor. The application must include the certificate of design compliance the surveyor signs, the plans it specifies and each technical certificate the surveyor relied on. An engineer's certificate can be one of those technical certificates.

On the **uncertified** route (form BA2), the council's own building surveyors assess the plans and issue the certificate of design compliance with the permit. This route is available only for Class 1a and Class 10 buildings, which covers houses and their outbuildings. City of Rockingham's uncertified checklist includes a copy of "structural engineers details" designed and certified by a Practising Structural Engineer.

The regulations set decision periods for both routes: 10 business days for certified and 25 business days for uncertified, or longer if agreed in writing, and the clock stops if the authority asks for more information. They don't include the time an engineer needs to produce the drawings, and they aren't a promise about any particular job.

The certificate that goes with the drawings deserves a note too. "Certificate of structural adequacy" is not a statutory WA form. The Building Act speaks of a "technical certificate", and councils use their own names. City of Perth's form is titled "Certificate of Structural Sufficiency". In it the signatory states the AS 2870 site class, the design gust wind speed, lists the documents the certificate covers, and confirms they qualify as a "Professional Engineer" as the Building Code defines that term. So a structural engineer's certificate (often called a certificate of structural adequacy or structural sufficiency) is tied to a specific set of drawings, and the drawings need to be identifiable enough to be listed.

A permit isn't guaranteed by good drawings. The building surveyor decides, and nobody can promise you an outcome.

## Standard drawings vs site-specific engineering drawings for a patio or shed

Picture a gable patio in Baldivis that the council won't permit without an engineer's certificate. The owners find an online seller of ready-made certified drawings for a few hundred dollars and wonder whether that's the same as an engineer looking at their block. It isn't the same. First, check whether you need a permit at all. A freestanding Class 10a building with a floor area up to 10 m², no higher than 2.4 m and outside wind regions C and D is exempt. So is a pergola with a Class 1 building, up to 2.4 m high, covering no more than 20 m², but the regulations define a pergola as a structure that does not have a roof. A roofed patio is not a pergola under that exemption.

Then compare what you'd be buying.

## How to check engineering drawings before you hand them over

Drawings get handed from engineer to owner to builder to surveyor, often without anyone reading them closely. A short check helps.

**Is it your house?** The address, lot number and your name should be on the title block. Generic drawings that could fit any house are the ones most likely to bounce back.

**Does it state the assumptions?** Look for the site class, the wind speed and the standards used. If a certificate refers to them, the drawings should be consistent with them.

**Does the certificate list the sheets?** Sheet numbers, revision letters and dates let a surveyor confirm they're reviewing the same set the engineer signed.

**Does it match the builder's plan?** If the architect or draftsperson moved a window after the engineer finished, the structural set may be out of date. A revision letter tells you which is current.

**Who signed it?** Ask for the engineer's name and qualifications. From 1 July 2027, check them against the DEMIRS register.

The last point matters most on extensions. A Bibra Lake family adding a second storey to a 1980s brick-and-tile house may be asking the existing walls and footings to carry more than they were built for, and the drawings have to deal with what the new floor will sit on. The engineer may need to see what's there before drawing anything. Any change in floor area or height takes the work outside the permit exemption, and so does replacing or underpinning footings. Our [extension engineering](/extension-engineering/) page covers the process, and no Perth firm publishes a price for extension design.

For illustration only, and not an estimate of what any extension costs, here's how the government charges behave when the estimated value is above $100,000. On $150,000 (GST included), a certified permit at 0.19% is $285, the Building Services Levy at 0.137% is $205.50, and the Construction Training Fund levy at 0.2% is $300. That last levy applies only above $100,000. None of that is an engineering fee. For how engineers price their work, see [structural engineer cost in Perth](/structural-engineer-cost-perth/).

Engineers assess and draw, and registered builders do the building work. The CSIRO [Foundation Maintenance and Footing Performance guide](https://research.csiro.au/infratech/wp-content/uploads/sites/38/2024/12/2979_FoundationMaintenanceandFootingPerformance_WCAG.pdf) is a sound place to read up on why footings move before you talk to anyone.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are engineering drawings the same as architectural plans?

No. Architectural or builder's plans show layout, rooms, windows and finishes. Engineering drawings show the structure: beams, posts, footings, connections and the assumptions behind them. Many jobs need both, and the engineer works from the plans, so any change to the plans after the engineer has finished can mean revised drawings.

### Do I need engineering drawings to remove a wall in Perth?

If the wall contributes to the support of another element, removing it is not exempt from a building permit. In that case the building surveyor will usually want engineering to go with the application. Whether your particular wall is load-bearing, and what would replace it, can only be worked out by an engineer who has seen the house.

### How much do engineering drawings cost in Perth?

Engineers quote each job. The price depends on the house, the structure and what the council needs, and no Perth engineering firm we found publishes a fee for wall removal, extension or site-specific patio drawings. The only published Perth figures are for standard details, such as A$330 for steel patio drawings and A$275 for certain wall details (GST not stated), and permit fees and levies are separate government charges.

### Will good engineering drawings get my permit approved?

No. The drawings support the application, but the building surveyor decides whether to sign the certificate of design compliance, and the permit authority decides the permit. What good drawings do is remove the obvious reasons for a request for more information.
