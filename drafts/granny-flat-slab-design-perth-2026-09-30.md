# Slab Design for a Granny Flat in Perth: The Engineering Side

A concrete slab is the one part of a granny flat you can't tweak once it's poured. If the slab design doesn't match the ground it sits on, the first sign is usually a door that binds or a stepped crack in a corner, and by then the walls are up and the fix is expensive. So the useful question isn't "how thick should the slab be". It's "what is the ground doing on this block, and has someone with the right qualifications written that down?"

This guide walks through the engineering side of slab design for a rear-yard granny flat (often called an ancillary dwelling): what gets decided, in what order, and what you should have in your hands before a builder pours anything. It won't tell you what your slab needs. That depends on your block, and only an independent structural engineer looking at it can say.

## What slab design for a granny flat actually decides

People talk about "the slab" as one thing. In practice the engineer is deciding several linked things at once.

- **The footing system.** Where the load-bearing edges and internal lines of the building sit, and how deep and stiff they need to be.
- **How much ground movement the slab must tolerate.** This comes from the AS 2870 site class (more on that below).
- **How wind loads reach the ground.** Wind calculations to AS/NZS 1170.2 feed into how the walls and roof are tied down to the slab.
- **What the paperwork says.** The building surveyor needs the design in a form they can rely on.

## Site class comes first, and the soil report is a separate job

Australian Standard AS 2870 sorts building sites into classes by how much the ground moves with moisture. Footing and slab design follows from the class. A slab that suits stable sand can be wrong on reactive clay, and the reverse is wasteful.

| Site class | What it means | Typical characteristic surface movement (ys) |
|---|---|---|
| A | Most sand and rock sites, little or no movement | Little or none |
| S | Slightly reactive clay | 0 to 20 mm |
| M | Moderately reactive clay or silt | 20 to 40 mm |
| H1 | Highly reactive clay | 40 to 60 mm |
| H2 | Highly reactive clay, very high movement | 60 to 75 mm |
| E | Extremely reactive clay | Above 75 mm |
| P | Soft soils, loose sands, landslip, collapsing or eroding soils, abnormal moisture, or sites that can't otherwise be classified | No movement range given |

The movement ranges come from a secondary source summarising AS 2870, not from the standard itself, so treat them as a guide to scale. Your engineer works from the standard and from your soil report.

Here is what matters for Perth. A Perth geotechnical firm says the most common soil type across the metro area is sand, normally Class A, that soft or loose sand sites are Class P, and that clay sites turn up "particularly in the Perth Hills". Geoscience Australia describes the Guildford Formation, a silty and slightly sandy clay, as outcropping over much of the eastern Perth region, and clayey alluvium on the Pinjarra Plain around Peel.
What nobody can do is tell you your class from your suburb. No source assigns a site class to a named suburb, and neighbouring blocks can differ. The class comes from testing on your block, and that testing is done by a geotechnical or soil-testing business, a different trade from structural engineering. Your engineer takes the classification as an input.

**Field scenario: a Kalamunda home on clay.** The owners of a Perth Hills block wanted a granny flat behind the main house. The soil report came back as one of the reactive clay classes, not Class A. A slab that would have suited sand was the wrong tool, and the engineer's design had to allow for seasonal swelling and shrinkage. The point isn't the exact detail. It's that the owners found out before the pour, from a report, rather than after a wet winter from sticking doors.

## Slab and footing types, in plain terms

You'll hear a few terms from builders and slab contractors. Here is what they mean without the jargon.

- **Slab-on-ground with edge and internal beams.** A concrete floor with thickened beams around the edge and along load lines. The beams stiffen the slab and carry wall loads down.
- **Stiffened raft.** A slab with a grid of deeper stiffening beams, used where the ground will move more. The stiffness lets the whole raft ride the movement instead of cracking.
- **Waffle-type systems.** Slabs cast over a grid of void formers to create a grid of beams. Usually a proprietary system with its own design tables, and the engineer still has to confirm it suits your site class.
None of these is "better". The right one depends on site class, wall loads, wind and what's already in the ground.

Concrete strength, reinforcement, beam depths and embedment all come from the engineer's design, and they depend on your site. Be wary of anyone who quotes a slab thickness or a reinforcement schedule before they know your site class. For the wider picture on adding to a house, the [footing design for extensions](/blog/footing-design-for-extensions/) guide covers the ground rules.

**Field scenario: a Joondalup rear yard with a soakwell in the way.** A family in the northern suburbs planned a granny flat where the main house's soakwell already sat. The City of Joondalup's rule is that a soakwell must be at least its own width away from buildings and boundaries, so excavation doesn't undermine footings. Moving the soakwell was the simpler fix, but only because it was spotted at the planning stage. Other councils have their own setbacks, and this article doesn't state them because they haven't been verified here. Check yours.

## Where Perth granny flat slabs go wrong

CSIRO's guide to foundation maintenance and footing performance is direct about what causes footing trouble. The causes that matter for a new slab are worth knowing before you build.

**Water near the footings.** CSIRO says a leaking water, sewer or stormwater pipe near a building can cause erosion, swelling or saturation, and that even a minuscule leak can be enough to saturate a clay foundation. It also notes that sandy soil is particularly susceptible to being washed away. Downpipes not connected to stormwater, and overwatering from automatic irrigation, are named as common culprits. For a granny flat this means the stormwater plan is part of the slab design, not an afterthought. Roof water spilling against a new edge beam undoes good engineering.

**Trees.** Roots can lift footings, or dry clay and make it shrink. A tree that has been harmless beside a garden shed for years can be a different neighbour to a slab. The [tree roots and cracking](/blog/tree-roots-cracking-house/) guide covers how that plays out.

**No drainage apron.** One of the remedies CSIRO lists is a paved apron at least 900 mm wide with a fall of 1:60, so water runs away from the building. It's a maintenance measure, but it's easy to plan in from the start.

**Field scenario: a Bibra Lake family and a damp patch.** A family adding a granny flat to a 1980s brick-and-tile noticed a persistently damp patch along the back fence, right where the new slab would sit. Nobody had looked at it because it was "just the garden". A plumber traced it to a leaking pipe. Sorting that out before excavation is far better than discovering it after the slab is in. If anything is wet near your build zone, get it checked first and tell the engineer.

## What the permit side needs from the engineer's paperwork

A granny flat adds floor area, which takes it outside the building permit exemption in Schedule 4 of the Building Regulations 2012. The same schedule says renovation isn't exempt where it involves underpinning or replacement of footings, which matters if you're joining onto an existing structure. Expect to need a building permit.

There are two routes in Western Australia.

- **Certified application.** A private building surveyor signs a certificate of design compliance, and the permit authority (your local government) decides within 10 business days, unless you agree to longer in writing.
- **Uncertified application.** Available only for Class 1a and Class 10 buildings. The local government's own surveyors assess it, with a decision period of 25 business days, again extendable by written agreement.

The clock stops if the authority asks for more information, so a complete package matters. The City of Rockingham's uncertified checklist, for one, asks for structural engineer's details designed and certified by a practising structural engineer. Other councils have their own lists.

The "certificate of structural adequacy" isn't a statutory form in Western Australia. The Building Act calls it a technical certificate, and councils use their own titles. The City of Perth's form, for example, is called a "Certificate of Structural Sufficiency". On it, the engineer states the AS 2870 site class, the design wind speed, the documents covered and their qualifications. In plain words, it's a structural engineer's certificate, often called a certificate of structural adequacy or structural sufficiency. Our [certificate of design compliance](/blog/certificate-of-design-compliance-wa/) guide explains where each document sits in the process.

Two legal points are worth knowing. First, from 1 July 2027 structural building engineering work in WA must be done by a registered building engineer, and unregistered work after that date is an offence. Some older web pages still say 1 July 2026, which has been superseded. Second, "engineer" and "structural engineer" aren't reserved titles today, so it's worth checking the credentials of whoever you hire. The WA Government's [building engineering registration guide](https://www.wa.gov.au/government/multi-step-guides/building-engineering-registration) explains the scheme and points to the register.

## What slab and footing design costs, and what nobody publishes

The honest answer is that no Perth engineering firm publishes a fee for footing and slab design. Engineers quote each job, and the price depends on the house, the structure and what the council needs. If a website gives you a firm price for your slab before it knows your site class, treat it with suspicion.

What you can work out is the government side. Permit fees are a percentage of the estimated value of the work, including GST. Here is arithmetic on an assumed build value of $150,000, which is an illustration and not a quote for anything.

- **Certified permit:** 0.19% of estimated value, minimum $121.00, so about $285.
- **Uncertified permit:** 0.32%, minimum $121.00, so about $480.
- **Building Services Levy:** 0.137% of value where the work is over $45,000, so about $205.50.
- **Construction Training Fund levy:** 0.2% of value, payable only where the value is over $100,000, so $300.

Those percentages come from the Building Regulations 2012 and the WA Government's building approvals page (last updated August 2026). Older council fee sheets still show a $110 minimum and a $20,000 levy threshold. Both are out of date.

Other items around the slab are priced by the businesses that supply them. As one data point, a Perth firm advertises compaction test certificates "from $198", with GST not stated. That's a testing product, not an engineering fee, and yours may differ.
### Five questions to ask before anything is poured

1. What site class was used, and where did it come from? You want a soil report, not an assumption.
2. Are the drawings site-specific, or a standard detail? Whether a council accepts a standard detail for a given site isn't something anyone can promise you.
3. What wind design was used? The certificate should say.
4. Where does the stormwater go? The slab drawings and the drainage plan should agree.
5. Who checks what before the pour? Excavations and reinforcement are jobs for a qualified person on site, not a phone call.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need an engineer for a granny flat slab in Perth?

A granny flat adds floor area, so it needs a building permit, and the application needs structural details. The City of Rockingham, for example, asks for engineer's details designed and certified by a practising structural engineer for uncertified applications. Confirm exactly what your local government and building surveyor want before you commission anything.

### What is a site class and why does it matter?

It's an AS 2870 rating of how much the ground moves with moisture, from Class A for most sand and rock sites up to E for extremely reactive clay, with P for soft or unclassifiable ground. The slab and footing design follows from it. It should come from testing on your block, not from your suburb.

### How much does slab and footing design cost?

No Perth engineering firm publishes a price for it. Engineers quote each job, and the cost depends on the site, the structure and what the council needs. Ask for a written quote and check what it includes, such as drawings, wind calculations and the certificate.

### Is the engineer's certificate the same as a certificate of structural adequacy?

Not exactly. That name isn't a statutory form in Western Australia, where the Building Act refers to a technical certificate and councils use their own titles. In practice it means a structural engineer's certificate that the building surveyor can rely on for the permit.
