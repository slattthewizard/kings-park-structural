# Soakwell Too Close to the House: How Water and Sand Crack Footings

Perth homes are built on sand more often than not, and the roof water usually goes into a soakwell in the yard. That works well until the soakwell is too close to the house. Then the water that is meant to disappear quietly into the ground starts moving the ground. This guide explains how a soakwell can affect a footing, what one council says about setbacks, which signs are worth taking seriously, and when an engineer helps.

## What a soakwell does

A soakwell is a pit, usually a perforated concrete ring or a modular tank, that takes stormwater from the roof and lets it soak into the soil. In sandy ground it can empty fast, which suits much of Perth.

The design relies on the water going down and out into the soil. What it does not control is where the water goes once it leaves the pit. If the soakwell is beside the footing, a lot of water is released very close to the structure that carries the house.

## How sand and footings interact

CSIRO's guidance on foundation maintenance and footing performance makes a point that matters for Perth: sandy soil is particularly susceptible to being washed away. Sand does not swell and shrink the way clay does, so people tend to think of it as stable. It is stable while it stays put. Moving water can carry fine grains out from under a footing, leaving a void or a loosened zone.

Three mechanisms are worth knowing.

**Concentrated water.** CSIRO notes that downpipes not connected to a stormwater system concentrate water next to footings. A soakwell fed by several downpipes is a concentrated source by design. During a heavy storm it receives the whole roof's runoff in a short time.

**Erosion of sand.** Water leaving the soakwell moves through the sand around it. If the flow path runs past a footing, fine material can be carried away over many seasons. CSIRO describes erosion in masonry as step cracking through the mortar and vertical cracks at openings, and says older masonry has little resistance to it.

**The excavation itself.** Digging a soakwell pit removes soil. If the pit is close to a footing, the soil beside the footing is gone. Sand does not stand up in a steep face, and a footing that has lost its side support can settle or move.

None of these needs a dramatic event. They work slowly and show up as cracks.

## Setbacks: what one council says

Councils set rules about how far a soakwell must be from buildings. This article can only report one. The City of Joondalup says a soakwell must sit at least the width of the soakwell away from buildings and boundaries, where the width is greater than or equal to the depth. The reason it gives is that the excavation should not undermine footings, fences or walls.

As a worked illustration of that rule, not a measurement for your site: if a soakwell is 1.2 m wide, the rule as the City states it would put it at least 1.2 m from the building. That is arithmetic on the City's wording, and your own council may say something different.

Other councils' setback rules are not something we could find stated, so we are not going to guess at them. Check your own council's page or ask its building or engineering team. If you are buying a house, ask whether the soakwells were installed with approval and where they sit.

## Signs worth taking seriously

No single sign proves a soakwell is the problem. Look for a pattern near the water source.

- **Stepped cracks in brickwork** running through the mortar, most obvious on the wall nearest the soakwell or a downpipe.
- **Vertical cracks at windows and doors** on that side of the house, where openings are the weak points.
- **Doors and windows that start to bind** on one side, or gaps opening at the top of a frame.
- **Cracks in a slab or paving** next to the house, or a path that has dropped away from the wall.
- **A depression or hole in the ground** near the soakwell or along the wall, which can mean soil is being carried away.
- **Water pooling or wet patches** beside the footing long after rain has stopped.

CSIRO says most cracking found in masonry walls is a cosmetic nuisance only, but also says monitoring should always be treated seriously. A hairline crack is usually just that. A crack that widens, or that comes with sticking doors on the same side as a soakwell, deserves a closer look.

Its damage categories give a rough sense of scale.

| Category | Approx. crack width | Typical damage |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Very slight | under 1 mm | Fine cracks, repair not usually needed |
| 2 Slight | under 5 mm | Noticeable, easily filled, doors may stick slightly |
| 3 Moderate | 5 to 15 mm | Some wall replacement possible, doors and windows stick |
| 4 Severe | 15 to 25 mm | Extensive repair, frames distort, walls lean or bulge |

Crack width is only one input. An engineer also looks at whether a crack is still moving, which is why dated photos with a tape measure in the frame are useful.

## What to check yourself

None of this is a diagnosis. It is a way of gathering the facts an engineer will ask for.

1. **Find the soakwell.** Look for a cover in the lawn or paving, and trace the downpipes. Measure how far it is from the nearest wall.
2. **Look at the downpipes and gutters.** A blocked gutter or a cracked pipe dumps water against the wall instead of into the system.
3. **Watch it in rain.** Does the soakwell overflow? Does water pool along the footing?
4. **Check the garden.** CSIRO lists overwatering from automatic systems as a common cause of saturation, so a sprinkler aimed at the wall counts.
5. **Photograph and date the cracks,** with a tape measure in the frame, and repeat every few weeks.
6. **Check for leaks.** CSIRO says even a very small leak from a water, sewer or stormwater pipe can saturate or erode the soil.

## When an engineer helps

Sort the problem into two kinds. A soakwell that is in the wrong place or overflowing, or a downpipe that needs connecting, is a drainage and plumbing job for a plumber or drainer. An engineer is not the right first call for that.

An engineer helps when the question is what the water has already done to the structure. That means:

- cracks that are widening or have passed the "easily filled" stage;
- cracks plus sticking doors or a sloping floor on the same side as the soakwell;
- a footing that you can see is exposed or undermined;
- a house you are about to buy or sell with cracks and a soakwell close to the wall.

An engineer decides what is moving, whether it is still moving, and what a fix has to deal with. That might include moving the soakwell, which is a separate job with its own approval questions, so confirm with your council before digging. Engineers assess and design. ## Insurance and who pays

Many home policies exclude gradual ground movement. One large WA insurer's product disclosure statement, dated 2021, excludes erosion, landslide, subsidence or movement unless directly caused by an earthquake, and excludes gradual leakage over time where the owner should have known. We treat that as an example of standard wording, not the current terms of any policy. Read your own policy and ask your insurer before assuming anything is or is not covered. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How close can a soakwell be to a house in Perth?

It depends on your council. The City of Joondalup says a soakwell must sit at least the width of the soakwell away from buildings and boundaries, where width is greater than or equal to depth. We could not find other councils' rules, so check your own council before installing or moving one.

### Can a soakwell crack the walls of a house?

It can contribute. Concentrated water in sand can wash fine material from beside or under a footing, and a pit dug close to a footing removes the side support. Cracks are a symptom, and an engineer is the person to say whether the soakwell is the cause or just one of several.

### How do I tell if cracks come from a soakwell or from something else?

You often cannot tell from the cracks alone. Leaking pipes, tree roots, overwatering and clay movement can produce similar patterns. Look for cracks that are worst on the wall nearest the soakwell or a downpipe, and ask an engineer to look at the whole picture.

### Should I move a soakwell that is close to my house?

Possibly, but get the cause established first. Moving a soakwell involves digging, and digging near a footing is where problems start. Check your council's setback rule, and speak to a plumber or drainer and, if there is cracking, an engineer before work begins.
