# Building on Limestone in Perth: What Cavities and Variable Rock Mean for Footings

Limestone sounds like the ideal thing to build on. It is rock, after all. The catch with Perth's coastal limestone is that it is not one uniform slab. It can be hard in one spot and weak or hollow a few metres away, and a footing needs to know which. This guide explains what the geology is, why variable ground matters to a footing, what site class P covers, and what coastal conditions do to some of the steel in a brick house.

## What the geology actually is

Geoscience Australia's review of Perth Basin geology gives the plain version. Tamala Limestone extends along the coastal strip of the Perth region. It is a calcareous dune rock, and the report describes it as having numerous solution channels and cavities. Those are spaces where rainwater has dissolved the limestone over a very long time, leaving channels and voids in the rock.

Above it, the same report describes the Spearwood Dune System as slightly calcareous aeolian sand lying over the Tamala Limestone. Aeolian just means wind-blown. Flanking the ocean are the Quindalup dunes. So a coastal site can be sand on top, rock below, and a younger dune system at the water's edge, all within a short distance.

That is where the public information ends. We have not found any source that maps which suburbs sit on limestone, so we will not say. Depth to rock, rock strength and the presence of cavities differ from lot to lot, and only an investigation of the actual site can say.

## Why variable ground is the problem

A footing spreads the weight of a house onto the ground. It works best when the ground under it behaves consistently. Uniform sand, or uniform rock, is predictable. The difficulty with limestone is the variation.

In general engineering terms, a few things follow.

- **Rock level can change across a house.** If rock is near the surface at one corner and deep at another, part of the footing bears on rock and part on sand or soil. Those two materials compress differently, which can lead to differential settlement.
- **Cavities and solution channels create voids.** A cavity under a footing is a place where the ground can give way or compress, now or later. The risk is a void that is not found before building.
- **Sand sitting in pockets can be loose.** Sand that has filled solution channels or lies in hollows in the rock may be softer than the surrounding material.
- **Excavation can be harder.** Digging footing trenches in rock is slower than digging in sand, and what is found when the trench is opened can change the design.

We are not giving figures for depths, strengths or spans, because none of the sources we use state them. What matters is the principle: the engineer needs to know what is actually under the footing, not what the area is generally like.

## Where site class P fits

AS 2870 sorts sites into classes. The definitions in the National Construction Code say Class A covers most sand and rock sites, with little or no movement from moisture changes. Class P covers soft soils, loose sands, landslip, collapsing soils, soils subject to erosion, abnormal moisture conditions, or sites that cannot otherwise be classified.

That wording matters for limestone in two ways. First, a site on sound rock or consistent sand may fall into Class A. Second, some of the conditions that limestone country can produce, such as loose sand pockets or a site that cannot otherwise be classified, are the sort of thing Class P describes. A Perth geotechnical firm makes the same general point: soft or loose sand sites are Class P.

So site class P covers some of the conditions you might meet on limestone, but it is not a label for limestone as such. Which class applies to your lot depends on a site investigation. A footing designed for the wrong class is the real risk. The class, along with the wind speed, is stated on the engineer's certificate that goes with a council application.

## What this means if you are building or extending

If you are adding to a house on or near limestone, the footing for the new part has to work alongside the footing of the old part. They may sit on different ground. See our guide to [footing design for extensions](/blog/footing-design-for-extensions/) for how that is handled in general, and [granny flat slab design in Perth](/blog/granny-flat-slab-design-perth/) if you are planning a slab on a separate structure.

Any change that adds floor area or height, or that involves underpinning or replacing footings, falls outside the building permit exemption in the Building Regulations 2012 (Schedule 4). That means a permit is needed, and the permit authority is your local council. Questions to put to whoever designs the work include:

1. What do we know about the ground under this lot, and how do we know it?
2. Has the ground been investigated, or are we assuming?
3. What happens to the design if the excavation turns up rock or a void?
4. Which site class is the design based on?

Soil testing and drilling are separate businesses from engineering. An engineer designs based on the information, and a builder builds. We do neither.

## Coastal conditions and steel in brickwork

Limestone country is mostly coastal, and the coast brings its own problem: salt air. A building-inspection firm in Western Australia has described coastal WA as the perfect rust storm for steel lintels, the beams over windows and doors in brick walls. It says rust expansion cracks the brickwork and mortar around the lintel, typically above windows and doors.

That is a secondary source, an inspection firm's view rather than a standard, so treat it as a reason to look rather than a rule. Our full guide to [rusted lintels in Perth](/blog/rusted-lintels-perth/) covers the signs and what an engineer checks. It is also worth remembering that a crack above a window is not always the lintel. Footing movement can crack the same spot, and an engineer is the person to tell the two apart.

## Cracks on a limestone-area house

A house on variable ground can crack in the same ways as any other, so there is no special limestone crack. What an owner can do is watch and record. Stepped cracks through mortar, vertical cracks at openings, doors that bind and cracks in a slab are all things to note with a date and a photo. Our guide to [cracks in concrete slabs](/blog/cracks-in-concrete-slab/) is useful if your floor is the concern, and [cracks in walls and when to worry](/blog/cracks-in-walls-when-to-worry/) explains when to escalate.

Do not assume that cracking near the coast is caused by limestone. Water is often the cause. CSIRO lists downpipes not connected to stormwater, overwatering and leaking pipes among the causes of ground saturation or erosion, and those apply everywhere.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is limestone a good foundation for a house?

It can be, but it depends on the lot. Tamala Limestone is described as having numerous solution channels and cavities, so rock can be sound in one place and hollow in another. The footing needs to be designed from what is actually under the site, not from the general reputation of rock.

### Does site class P mean limestone?

No. Class P is for soft soils, loose sands, landslip, collapsing soils, erosion-prone soils, abnormal moisture conditions or sites that cannot otherwise be classified. Some conditions found on limestone country could fall under it, but the class for any lot comes from a site investigation.

### Which Perth suburbs are on limestone?

We cannot say. Geoscience Australia describes Tamala Limestone along the coastal strip of the Perth region, but we have not found a source that maps individual suburbs. The ground can differ between neighbouring lots, so check your own site.

### Does salt air damage a brick house?

It can affect steel inside the brickwork. A Western Australian inspection firm describes coastal conditions as hard on steel lintels, with rust expansion cracking the brick and mortar around them. That is a secondary source, so it is a reason to have a cracked lintel looked at, not proof of a fault.
