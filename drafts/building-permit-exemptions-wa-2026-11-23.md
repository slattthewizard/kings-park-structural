# Do I Need a Building Permit in WA? What Schedule 4 Exempts and What It Does Not

You are about to build a shed, a pergola or a retaining wall, or knock something out inside, and you want to know whether you can start. The rule in Western Australia sits in Schedule 4 of the Building Regulations 2012, which lists the work that is exempt from a building permit. The list is short and the conditions on each item are tight. Miss one and the exemption is gone.

This guide goes through the items that matter most to home owners, in plain language. It is a summary of the regulation, not legal advice, and your local council has the last word on how it applies to your block.

## The starting point

Building work generally needs a building permit. Schedule 4 lists the exceptions. If your job fits an exception exactly, no permit is needed. If it does not, a permit is. There is no middle ground where a job is almost exempt.

Local government is the permit authority for houses, so your council is the right place to confirm the answer for your site.

## Item 1: a small freestanding shed or similar

A freestanding Class 10a building is exempt if it meets all of these conditions:

- the floor area does not exceed 10 square metres
- it is no more than 2.4 metres high
- it is not in wind region C or D under AS 1170.2

The words that trip people up are "freestanding" and the wind condition. A shed attached to the house is not freestanding. The wind region depends on where the site is, so check it rather than assuming. If your shed is bigger than 10 square metres, or taller than 2.4 metres, the exemption does not apply. [Patio and shed certificates](/patio-shed-certificates/) covers what the engineering side looks like when it does not.

## Item 7: a pergola

A pergola associated with a Class 1 building is exempt if:

- it covers an area not exceeding 20 square metres
- it is no more than 2.4 metres high
- it is not in wind region C or D

The definition is the catch. A pergola, for this item, is a structure that does not have a roof. It may have an open-weave or permeable covering, but it cannot have a roof. A roofed patio is therefore not a pergola under this item, however much it looks like one. If you want a solid roof over the outdoor area, the pergola exemption is not available. [What an engineering certificate for a patio or shed involves](/blog/engineering-certificate-patio-shed/) explains the next step.

## Item 6: a retaining wall

A retaining wall that retains no more than 0.5 metres of soil is exempt, provided it is not associated with other building work. The second condition matters. A low wall built as part of a larger job, such as an extension, is part of that job, not a separate exempt item. A taller wall is outside the exemption altogether.

## Item 2: renovations and repairs

This is the item that catches the most people, because it sounds generous. Renovation or alteration work is exempt only if it does not affect the structural soundness of the building, and only if it does not include any of these:

- an increase or decrease in floor area or height
- underpinning or replacement of footings
- removal or alteration of an element that contributes to the support of any other element

Work that touches none of those three points may stay inside the exemption. Taking out a wall that holds up the roof or an upper floor is not. Adding a room is not. Replacing footings is not. These three limits are the ones that matter for most owners considering structural change, and they connect directly to the engineering topics on this site: [wall removal](/wall-removal-engineering/), [extensions](/extension-engineering/) and footing work.

Item 2 also has a heritage carve-out, in paragraph 2(g). Work subject to a heritage protection order, heritage works permit, repair notice or repair order is excluded from the repair exemption. If your house is heritage listed, assume the exemption does not cover you until the council says otherwise. Some councils maintain their own lists and areas, so check yours.

## Quick reference

| Work | Exempt if | Not exempt if |
|---|---|---|
| Freestanding Class 10a (shed) | 10 m2 or less, 2.4 m high or less, not wind region C or D | Larger, taller, attached, or in wind region C or D |
| Pergola | 20 m2 or less, 2.4 m or less, no roof, not wind region C or D | Has a roof (a roofed patio), larger, or in wind region C or D |
| Retaining wall | Retains 0.5 m or less, not part of other building work | Retains more, or part of a bigger job |
| Renovation | No effect on structural soundness, no change in floor area or height, no footing work, no support element removed | Any of those |

This table is a summary. The regulation has the full wording, and the conditions combine, so a job has to meet all of the conditions listed for its item.

## Exempt from a permit does not mean exempt from the rules

Being exempt from a building permit is not the same as being exempt from the rules. An exempt shed is still a building. It still has to be built safely, and other requirements, such as planning rules and property boundaries, still apply.

It is a mistake to treat the exemption as a green light. Ask your council what else applies before you start. A short phone call costs nothing and avoids the expensive outcome, which is a notice to deal with work that should have had approval. The process for work already done without approval is separate again: the owner applies for a building approval certificate, and the fee for unauthorised work is 0.38% of the estimated value, with a minimum of $121.00.

## Planning approval is separate

A building permit and planning approval are two different things. Planning looks at where and how you build on the block, and the answer varies by council. This guide does not state planning rules. A job can be exempt from a building permit and still need planning approval, so ask the council about both.

## When a permit is needed

If your job is outside the exemptions, the application goes to your local council. A certified application (BA1) is checked by a registered private building surveyor, and an uncertified one (BA2) by the council's own building surveyors, which is available only for Class 1a and Class 10 buildings. The statutory decision periods are 10 business days for certified and 25 for uncertified, and the clock stops if more information is requested.

Government fees are set by regulation. A certified building permit for Class 1 or Class 10 is 0.19% of estimated value, with a minimum of $121.00. An uncertified permit is 0.32%, with the same minimum. As an illustration only, on $60,000 of work, 0.19% would be $114, so the $121.00 minimum applies. You can read the current table on the [WA Government building approvals page](https://www.wa.gov.au/organisation/building-and-energy/building-approvals). For the engineering side of an extension, see [extension engineering](/extension-engineering/).

Much of what a permit application needs, such as footings, structural details and wind design, comes from an engineer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need a building permit for a shed in WA?

Not always. A freestanding Class 10a building of no more than 10 square metres, no more than 2.4 metres high and outside wind region C or D is exempt. Anything larger, taller or in those wind regions needs a permit, so check with your council.

### Is a patio the same as a pergola for permit purposes?

No. For the exemption, a pergola has no roof, though it may have an open-weave covering. A roofed patio is not a pergola, so the pergola exemption does not apply to it.

### Do I need a permit for a retaining wall?

A retaining wall that holds back no more than 0.5 metres and is not associated with other building work is exempt. A taller wall, or one built as part of a bigger job, is not.

### If my work is exempt, can I ignore the council?

No. Being exempt from a building permit is not the same as being exempt from the rules. Planning approval is separate, and other requirements may still apply, so ask your local council before you start.
