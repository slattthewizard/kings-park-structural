# Buying a House With Cracks in Perth: What to Check and Who Can Check It

You are standing in a kitchen you like, and there is a diagonal line running up from the corner of the window. The agent says it is just settling. Maybe so. This guide does not tell you whether to buy, because nothing here can. What it does is lay out the checks that are worth doing before you sign, the difference between the two kinds of report people mix up, and a few points about approvals and insurance that cracked houses often raise.

## Two different reports, two different jobs

A pre-purchase building inspection and an engineer's assessment of a crack are not the same product. People buy the first and assume they have the second.

A pre-purchase report is typically written to AS 4349.1 by a building inspector. It surveys the whole house: roof, ceilings, walls, floors, timber pests, wet areas, and whatever defects are visible. If it spots cracking, it will usually note it and may recommend further advice.

An engineer's assessment of specific cracks goes narrower and deeper. It asks what is moving, why, whether it is still moving and what would be involved in dealing with it. One Perth firm states plainly that its inspection reports do not include structural design, engineering drawings or engineering certification. That is a legitimate product, but it is a different one. Our guide on [structural inspection vs building inspection](/blog/structural-inspection-vs-building-inspection/) covers the difference in more detail.

One sequence is the building inspection first, then an engineer on whatever it flags. The timing matters, because contracts often have limited periods for conditions.

## Reading the cracks yourself first

You can learn a lot by walking the house slowly before you pay for anything. CSIRO's guide to foundation maintenance and footing performance is the standard plain-English reference, and it classes cracks by width.

| Category | Approx. width | What it tends to look like |
|---|---|---|
| 0 Negligible | under 0.1 mm | Hairline |
| 1 Very slight | under 1 mm | Fine, not needing repair |
| 2 Slight | under 5 mm | Noticeable; doors and windows may stick slightly |
| 3 Moderate | 5 to 15 mm | Doors and windows stick; pipes can fracture |
| 4 Severe | 15 to 25 mm | Frames distort; walls lean or bulge |

CSIRO also says most cracking found in masonry walls is a cosmetic nuisance only, while monitoring should always be treated seriously. A photo in a listing cannot tell you which side of that your house is on.

### Patterns worth noticing

- **Stepped cracks through the mortar**, and vertical cracks at window and door openings. CSIRO links these to erosion or movement in masonry.
- **Doors and windows that bind**, or gaps opening at cornices and between walls and ceilings. On clay these can point to seasonal swelling and shrinkage.
- **Cracks that follow a line through several rooms**, rather than one isolated spot.
- **Wet patches, damp smells or a downpipe that discharges beside the wall.** Water is a frequent culprit: CSIRO lists leaking pipes, unconnected downpipes, over-watering and tree roots.
- **Patched and freshly painted sections**, especially in one corner. Ask what was done and when.
- **Cracks in the slab or the floor tiles**.

Photograph what you see with a tape measure in frame. If you can visit twice, at different times, a second look will show whether anything has changed.

## Check what has been built or removed

Cracks sometimes come from something the owner changed. A wall taken out between kitchen and living room, or a patio added on the back, can leave the structure doing something it was not designed to do. In a full-masonry house, CSIRO says at least some internal walls usually carry upper floors, ceilings and roof, and removing a supporting element is outside the building permit exemption. For an overview, see [removing a load-bearing wall in Perth](/blog/removing-a-load-bearing-wall-perth/).

So ask for the approvals. Does the council have a permit for the alterations? Look for a gap between the house as drawn and the house as built, such as a missing wall, an enlarged opening or an added room. If work was done without approval, owners regularise it with a building approval certificate, form BA13. The route involves the council, a registered building surveyor, a certificate of building compliance (BA18) and typically a structural engineer's inspection and certification. The government fee for a building approval certificate for unauthorised work is 0.38% of the estimated value, not less than $121.00, plus the Building Services Levy. As an illustration only: on $50,000 of work, 0.38% would be $190. Those figures do not include the engineer's or surveyor's fees, which are not published. The wa.gov.au page on [building approvals](https://www.wa.gov.au/organisation/building-and-energy/building-approvals) lists the current fees.

Who carries that cost when you buy is something to raise with whoever is advising you on the contract.

Patios and sheds are a separate question: see [patio and shed certificates](/patio-shed-certificates/).

## Insurance and cracks

Home insurance policies vary, and the policy you will hold after settlement is the one that matters. As an example of standard wording, one large WA insurer's product disclosure statement dated 25 February 2021 (we have not confirmed it is the current edition) excludes erosion, landslide, subsidence or movement unless directly caused by an earthquake, and excludes defects in design or material and gradual leakage over time in some circumstances.

Many home policies exclude gradual subsidence and ground movement. Check your own policy's exclusions, and ask your insurer what they would want to see before you buy. We do not know how any insurer will treat a particular house, and a claim may or may not be paid. See [subsidence insurance in WA](/blog/subsidence-insurance-wa/) for more.

## The questions to put to the seller

None of these guarantees an answer. They show you how much the seller knows.

1. When did the cracks first appear, and have they changed?
2. Has anyone looked at them? Is there a report you can read?
3. What repairs or patching have been done?
4. Were any walls, openings or extensions built or altered, and are there approvals?
5. Are there known leaks, tree removals or drainage works?

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I get a building inspection or an engineer's report before buying a house with cracks?

They do different things. A pre-purchase building inspection is a wide survey by a building inspector. An engineer's assessment looks at specific cracks and what is causing them. One approach is to use the first to find the problems and the second to investigate any cracking it flags.

### Can a crack in a house be harmless?

CSIRO says most cracking in masonry walls is a cosmetic nuisance only, and it also says monitoring should always be treated seriously. Width, pattern and whether the crack is growing all matter. Nobody can promise a crack is harmless from a photograph.

### What if the house has unapproved renovations?

Unauthorised work can be regularised with a building approval certificate (form BA13) through the council. The process involves a registered building surveyor and usually a structural engineer's inspection. Fees and timing vary, so ask the council before relying on it.

### Will my home insurance cover cracks from subsidence?

Many home policies exclude gradual subsidence and ground movement. Check your own policy's exclusions and ask the insurer directly. We cannot say whether any claim would be paid.
