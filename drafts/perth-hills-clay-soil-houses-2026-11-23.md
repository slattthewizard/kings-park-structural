# Perth Hills Clay Soil: Why It Moves and What It Does to a House

Most of Perth sits on sand, so most owners never think about what the ground is doing under their house. In the hills and the eastern suburbs the picture changes. If your doors stick every winter and free off by summer, or a crack opens in autumn and half closes by spring, the ground may be changing size under you. This guide explains where clay shows up around Perth, how reactive clay behaves, and what an engineer looks at when a house on clay starts to crack.

## Where clay turns up around Perth

Geoscience Australia's review of Perth Basin geology describes the Guildford Formation as predominantly brown silty and slightly sandy clay, up to 35 m thick. Its type area is the Swan Valley, and it outcrops over much of the eastern Perth region. At the foot of the Darling Scarp, the foothills are colluvial slopes, ground that has washed down from higher up the hill.

A Perth geotechnical firm makes the contrast with the rest of the city. It says the most common soil type in the Perth metropolitan area is sand, normally Class A, and that clay sites occur particularly in the Perth Hills.

That is as precise as the public sources allow. No source we have found assigns a soil type or a site class to a named suburb, so we will not either. Two houses in the same street can sit on different ground, and the only way to know what is under yours is a site investigation commissioned for your lot. If you are in the City of Swan, the City of Kalamunda or the Shire of Mundaring, clay is a reasonable thing to ask about. It is not something to assume.

## What makes clay different from sand

Sand drains fast and moves little with moisture. Its main risk is erosion, where water carries it away. Clay holds water, and it changes volume as the moisture content changes. It swells when wet and shrinks when dry.

CSIRO's footing guidance describes the result. Seasonal swelling and shrinkage cause the ground surface to dish or dome, and the building follows. Doors bind and cornices crack. Because soil near the edges of a house is exposed to rain and sun while soil under the middle is more sheltered, the two zones move differently. That difference is what flexes the footing.

### Dishing and doming

Think of two shapes:

- **Doming** is when the middle of the house ends up higher than the edges. Typically the soil around the edges dries and shrinks while the ground under the centre, sheltered from sun and rain, stays wetter. The edges drop.
- **Dishing** is the reverse: the edges end up higher than the middle. Typically the soil at the edges swells after wet weather while the centre changes less. The edges lift.

A house does not simply sit in one shape. Wet winters and dry summers flex it back and forth, and a long dry spell can push the doming further than usual. Over many cycles, brickwork, which cannot stretch much, cracks. CSIRO describes cracking in masonry as often cosmetic, but says it should always be treated seriously when you monitor it. That is a sensible attitude to take with clay.

## How classes describe it

Australian Standard AS 2870 sorts building sites into classes by how much the ground is expected to move with moisture. The definitions, as reproduced in the National Construction Code, run like this:

| Class | Ground | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| A | Most sand and rock sites | Little or no movement from moisture changes |
| S | Slightly reactive clay | Slight movement |
| M | Moderately reactive clay or silt | Moderate movement |
| H1, H2 | Highly reactive clay | High and very high movement |
| E | Extremely reactive clay | Extreme movement |
| P | Soft soils, loose sands and other problem sites | Abnormal conditions, or a site that cannot otherwise be classified |

Footing design depends on the class. A footing designed for a Class A sand site is not the same as one designed for a highly reactive clay site, and the engineer's certificate for a council application states the site class. That is why a new extension on a clay site needs more thought than one on sand. See our guide to [footing design for extensions](/blog/footing-design-for-extensions/) for how that plays out.

The class for your lot is something a site investigation determines. Nothing in this article tells you what class your house or your suburb falls into.

## Trees, gardens and drainage on clay

Clay reacts to moisture, so anything that changes moisture near the house matters more on a clay site. CSIRO lists several:

- **Trees.** Roots can lift footings, or they can dry out the clay and cause it to shrink. A large tree close to a wall pulls water out of the soil, and the ground on that side shrinks. The effect is worst in a dry stretch.
- **Garden watering.** CSIRO notes that misusing automatic watering systems is a common cause of saturation.
- **Roof water.** Downpipes not connected to a stormwater system concentrate water beside the footings.
- **Leaks.** CSIRO says even a minuscule leak can be enough to saturate a clay foundation.

The common thread is keeping moisture around the house as even as you can. A tree that is drying one side and a leaking pipe wetting the other is a recipe for exactly the uneven movement that cracks walls. Our guide to [tree roots and cracking houses](/blog/tree-roots-cracking-house/) goes into the detail.

## Reading the signs on a clay site

Clay movement tends to show up in recognisable ways. None of these proves a clay problem alone, and each can have other causes.

- Doors and windows that stick in one season and free off in another
- Cracks that open and close with the seasons rather than only growing
- Cracking at the corners of windows and doors, where the wall is weakest
- Cornices that crack or separate from the ceiling
- Floors or a slab that appear to rise in the middle or fall at the edges

Cracks that change with the season are a strong clue that moisture is involved, but the clue cuts both ways. A crack that keeps widening year after year is not just seasonal flexing. Record what you see. Our short guide to [cracks in walls and when to worry](/blog/cracks-in-walls-when-to-worry/) lists what to track and when to escalate.

## What an engineer does, and what they do not

An independent structural engineer looks at the house, not at the clay in isolation. They check the pattern and width of cracks, whether doors and windows bind, whether there are signs of tree or water influence, and whether the movement looks finished or ongoing. They may recommend investigation of the ground, drainage corrections, tree management or monitoring, depending on what they find. Soil testing, underpinning and building work are separate businesses. Engineers assess and design; builders build, and we do none of it.

If the house has moved, the fix depends on the cause. Where drainage or a tree is the cause, fixing that often comes first. Where footing work is needed, replacing or underpinning footings is not exempt work under the Building Regulations 2012, so a permit is needed. Check with your council or building surveyor.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is the ground in the Perth Hills all clay?

No. A Perth geotechnical firm says clay sites occur particularly in the Perth Hills, but that is a general statement, not a map. Geoscience Australia describes colluvial slopes at the foot of the Darling Scarp and Guildford Formation clay over much of the eastern Perth region. Whether your lot has clay depends on a site investigation of that lot.

### What is dishing and doming?

They are the two shapes a house on reactive clay can take as the soil moisture changes. Doming leaves the middle of the house higher than the edges, usually when the edge soil dries and shrinks. Dishing is the reverse, with the edges higher than the middle, usually when the edge soil swells after wet weather. The flexing between the two is what cracks brickwork and makes doors bind.

### Can I find out my site class from my suburb?

No. No source we found assigns an AS 2870 site class to a named suburb, and ground can differ between neighbouring lots. The class for a specific house comes from a site investigation for that lot.

### Do trees really matter on clay?

Yes. CSIRO says roots can lift footings or dry the clay and cause it to shrink. A tree close to the house can dry the ground on one side more than the other, which adds to uneven movement. An engineer can judge whether a tree is a likely contributor to cracking at your house.
