# What Does a Structural Engineer Do for a Home Owner in Perth?

You've got a builder's quote for an open-plan kitchen, a hairline crack over the lounge window, or a council letter asking for "engineer's details", and somebody has said you need a structural engineer. Before you pay for anything, it helps to know what does a structural engineer do, what they hand back, and when the honest answer is that you don't need one yet.

In short, a structural engineer works out how a building holds itself up and whether a change or a defect affects that. They assess what's there, calculate the loads, and put the answer in writing so a builder, a building surveyor or an insurer can act on it. They don't build anything.

## What a structural engineer actually does

Every house is a chain of loads. Roof, ceiling and upper floors push down onto walls and beams, the walls push down onto footings, and the footings spread that weight into the ground. An engineer's job is to check that chain, or to redesign part of it when you want to change it.

In practice that comes down to four kinds of work.

- **Assessing what's already there.** Walls, roof framing, floors, slab and footings, and signs of cracking, sagging or shifting.
- **Designing the fix or the change.** A steel or LVL beam where a wall comes out, a footing for an extension, the bracing for a patio.
- **Producing documents.** Calculations, drawings and reports that a building surveyor can rely on.
- **Certifying.** A signed structural engineer's certificate, often called a certificate of structural adequacy or structural sufficiency, for a council application.

That last item needs a note. "Certificate of structural adequacy" is not a statutory form in Western Australia. The Building Act 2011 talks about a "technical certificate", and councils use their own names and forms. The City of Perth's form, for example, is titled a Certificate of Structural Sufficiency. So don't be surprised if your council or surveyor asks for the same thing under a different name.

## Knocking out a wall: is it holding something up?

This is the job that starts most enquiries. Double brick is common in Perth: an HIA-commissioned report cited by ABC News in June 2026 found Western Australia was the only state where more than half of detached houses were brick, at 68 per cent. CSIRO notes that in full-masonry houses, the inner leaf of the external walls and at least some internal walls usually carry the upper floors, ceiling and roof. So an internal wall in a brick house can be structural even when it looks like a divider.

Picture a 1970s double-brick home in Duncraig where the owners want the kitchen wall gone. The engineer checks what sits on the wall (ceiling joists, roof trusses, or nothing at all), designs a steel or LVL beam if the wall is carrying load, and specifies the posts and footings that take the load down at each end. Temporary propping while the wall comes out is a builder's job, but the engineer's design tells the builder what the props are protecting.

Whether the wall can come out at all depends on what the engineer finds. No one can tell you from a photo that it's safe, and this page won't either. Under the Building Regulations 2012, removing an element that supports another element is not exempt work, so it needs a building permit, and the surveyor will want the engineering with the application.

If you want to try a first pass yourself, our guide on [how to tell if a wall is load-bearing](/blog/how-to-tell-if-a-wall-is-load-bearing/) covers what to look for. It won't replace the engineer, but it tells you whether to make the call. Our [wall removal engineering](/wall-removal-engineering/) page explains the process from enquiry to design.

## Cracks: serious or just cosmetic?

The second reason people search this term is a crack. Perth houses move, and most movement is small. CSIRO's guidance, which reproduces the Australian Standard AS 2870 damage categories, says most cracking in masonry walls is a cosmetic nuisance only. It also says monitoring should always be treated seriously.

The categories run from 0 to 4, and crack width is the first thing an engineer measures.

| Category | Approx. crack width | What it typically looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 0 Negligible | Under 0.1 mm | Hairline cracks |
| 1 Very slight | Under 1 mm | Fine cracks that don't need repair |
| 2 Slight | Under 5 mm | Noticeable, easily filled; doors and windows may stick slightly |
| 3 Moderate | 5 to 15 mm, or several cracks of 3 mm or more in a group | Doors and windows stick; pipes can fracture |
| 4 Severe | 15 to 25 mm | Frames distort; walls lean or bulge |

Width is only part of the story. The engineer also looks at the pattern, whether the crack is growing, and what's causing it.

Take a stepped crack above a window in a Rockingham house two streets from the beach. Stepped cracks that follow the mortar joints can point to movement in the footing or to a lintel problem. Coastal salt air is hard on steel lintels, and a WA building-inspection firm describes rust expansion cracking the brickwork around the lintel, typically above windows and doors. That's a secondary source, so treat it as a lead for the engineer to check, not a diagnosis.

Or take a Kalamunda home on clay whose back doors started sticking after a wet winter. Clay is documented in the eastern suburbs and the Perth Hills (the Guildford Formation is brown silty and slightly sandy clay), and clay swells and shrinks with moisture. CSIRO lists leaking pipes, downpipes that dump water beside the footing, over-watering from automatic systems, and tree roots among the usual causes. An engineer tries to work out which one you have before anyone spends money on a repair.

Nothing here tells you a particular crack is harmless, and no article can. If you're worried, [tell us what you're seeing](/#contact). ## Extensions, second storeys and footings

Adding floor area or height takes a job outside the building-permit exemption, so extensions and second storeys need a permit. Uncertified applications (assessed by the council's own surveyors) are only available for Class 1a and Class 10 buildings. City of Rockingham's checklist for that route asks for structural engineer's details designed and certified by a practising structural engineer.

Think of a Bibra Lake family adding a second storey to a 1980s brick-and-tile. The engineer has to answer two questions. Can the existing walls carry the extra floor? And can the existing footings carry the extra load, or do they need work? Replacing or underpinning footings is not exempt work either, and it's building work that a registered builder does. The engineer assesses and designs; someone else builds.

Footing design also depends on the AS 2870 site class. Class A covers most sand and rock sites. Reactive clays fall into the S, M, H and E classes, and Class P covers soft or loose soils. A Perth geotechnical firm says sand is the most common soil in the metropolitan area, with clay sites particularly in the Perth Hills. We can't give you a site class for your street from a web page. The site class comes from testing on your block.

For a granny flat, the same logic applies to the slab, but planning limits on ancillary dwellings are a council and state planning matter that this page doesn't cover.

## Patios, carports and sheds

A patio looks like the simplest job on this list, and it's where owners hit the paperwork wall. A freestanding Class 10a building up to 10 m² and 2.4 m high can be exempt from a building permit, and so can a pergola up to 20 m² and 2.4 m high. But the regulations define a pergola as a structure that does not have a roof. A solid-roofed patio isn't a pergola, so the exemption is easy to misread.

Now picture a gable patio in Baldivis the council will not permit without an engineer's certificate. The engineer needs the site class under AS 2870 and the design wind speed under AS/NZS 1170.2, and the certificate lists the drawings it covers. If your patio is attached to the house or is bigger than the exemption allows, expect the surveyor to want that certificate.

One shortcut exists. Some Perth firms sell standard, not site-specific, certified drawings online. One lists a steel patio drawing at A$330 (GST not stated) and screen and retaining wall details at A$275 each. Whether a council accepts a standard detail for your block isn't something we can confirm, so check with your surveyor before you buy one. Our guide to the [engineering certificate for a patio or shed](/blog/engineering-certificate-patio-shed/) covers the trade-offs, and the [patio and shed certificates](/patio-shed-certificates/) page explains what to send.

## What it costs, and what the figures actually mean

No Perth engineering firm we could find publishes a fee for an engineer's crack report, beam design, site-specific patio certificate or extension design. Engineers quote each job. The price depends on the house, the structure and what the council needs. Anyone who quotes you a flat number before seeing photos or plans is guessing.

The Perth figures that do exist belong to other products, and it's worth reading them for what they are.

| Figure | What it actually is |
|---|---|
| From $440 including GST | A pre-purchase building inspection report by a licensed building inspector, not an engineer's report |
| About $495 including GST | A firm's approximate price for a building inspection report on an average Perth home |
| A$330 (GST not stated) | A standard steel patio drawing, not site-specific |
| 0.19% of estimated value, minimum $121.00 | Council permit fee for a certified application, Class 1 or 10 |
| 0.32% of estimated value, minimum $121.00 | Council permit fee for an uncertified application |

A building inspection and an engineer's report are different things. Building inspectors check condition and defects to AS 4349.1; an engineer assesses structural adequacy and designs solutions. If someone has offered you a "structural inspection", ask who's doing it and what the report will say. Our comparison of a [structural inspection vs a building inspection](/blog/structural-inspection-vs-building-inspection/) sets out the difference.

The permit and levy figures matter as well. The Building Services Levy is 0.137% of value above $45,000 (or $61.65 at or below it), and the Construction Training Fund levy is 0.2% but only where the work is over $100,000.

## Do you need one? And what to check on the engineer

Sometimes the first step is to find out whether you need an engineer at all. A hairline crack that hasn't changed, in a house with no other signs, may only need watching and a dated photo. Fixing a downpipe or a leaking pipe may come before anything else. An engineer becomes the right call when the crack is widening, when doors and windows are binding, when you want to remove or alter a wall, or when the permit application asks for engineering.

On the engineer side, the law in Western Australia is changing. "Engineer" and "structural engineer" are not reserved titles at the moment. From 1 July 2027, structural building engineering work must be done by a registered building engineer, and unregistered work becomes an offence. The Department of Energy, Mines, Industry Regulation and Safety (DEMIRS) keeps a Building Engineer Register you can check at [dmirs.wa.gov.au](https://www.dmirs.wa.gov.au/). Ask the engineer who quotes your job for their registration details and check them.

For the underlying legislation, WA statutes are published at [legislation.wa.gov.au](https://www.legislation.wa.gov.au/). For cracking and soil movement, CSIRO's Building Technology File on foundation maintenance and footing performance is the standard reference, available from [csiro.au](https://www.csiro.au/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does a structural engineer do on a home renovation?

They check whether the existing walls, footings and roof can carry the changes you want, then design the beams, footings or bracing needed and put it in writing for the building surveyor. They don't do the building. A registered builder does that.

### Do I need a structural engineer to remove a wall?

If the wall is load-bearing, you will almost certainly need engineering for the beam and the permit application, because removing a supporting element isn't exempt work. If the wall carries nothing, you may not, but you need someone qualified to confirm that first.

### How much does a structural engineer cost in Perth?

Perth engineers quote each job and don't publish fees for reports, beam design or certificates. The price depends on the house, the structure and what the council needs. Published Perth figures such as $440 to $495 are for building inspections, which are a different product.

### Is a structural engineer the same as a building inspector?

No. A building inspector reports on condition and visible defects, often as a pre-purchase report under AS 4349.1. A structural engineer assesses structural adequacy and designs solutions such as beams and footings. Some jobs need one, some need the other, and some need both.
