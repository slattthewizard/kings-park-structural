# Sticking Doors and Windows: When It Points to House Movement

A door that used to swing shut now catches at the top corner. A window that slid easily last year needs a shove. Most of the time the cause is dull: timber that has taken up moisture, a loose hinge, a layer too many of paint. Sometimes it is the house moving. This guide explains how to tell the likely causes apart, what to write down, and when the pattern is worth an engineer's attention.

## Why a door or window sticks at all

A door sits in a frame, and the frame sits in a wall. If any of the three changes shape, the clearance around the door changes and it starts to rub. The change can come from the door, the frame, or the structure behind both.

Three groups of cause cover most cases.

**The door or window itself.** Timber takes up moisture and swells, then dries and shrinks. A door that sticks in a humid spell and frees up later is behaving like a piece of timber. So is one that binds on a single edge after a wet week. Hinges that have worked loose let the door drop slightly, so it rubs the frame at the latch side. Layers of paint on the edge can do the same.

**The frame.** A frame can twist if the wall around it has moved a little, or if it was never fitted square.

**The building.** When the footings under part of a house move, the walls above them move with them. Openings are the weak points in a wall, so doors and windows are where the movement shows. CSIRO's guide to foundation maintenance and footing performance notes that on clay soils, seasonal swelling and shrinkage of the ground cause the footings to dish and dome, and that doors bind and cornices crack as a result.

The first two groups are maintenance jobs. The third is why this page exists.

## What CSIRO says about doors in its damage categories

CSIRO reproduces the AS 2870 crack categories, which grade damage by crack width and by what else is happening to the building. Doors and windows turn up in the middle of that scale.

| Category | Approx. crack width | Typical damage |
|---|---|---|
| 0 Negligible | under 0.1 mm | Hairline cracks |
| 1 Very slight | under 1 mm | Fine cracks that do not need repair |
| 2 Slight | under 5 mm | Cracks noticeable and easily filled; doors and windows stick slightly |
| 3 Moderate | 5 to 15 mm, or several cracks of 3 mm or more in one group | Some wall replacement possible; doors and windows stick; pipes can fracture |
| 4 Severe | 15 to 25 mm, also depends on number of cracks | Extensive repair; frames distort; walls lean or bulge |

The table does not say a sticking door means category 2 or 3. It says that when cracks of those sizes are present, sticking doors and windows tend to come with them. A door that sticks with no cracking anywhere sits outside this table altogether, and the timber and hinge causes are more likely.

CSIRO also says most cracking found in masonry walls is a cosmetic nuisance only, but that monitoring should always be treated seriously. Most cracks do not mean trouble. The ones that grow are the reason you watch.

## Telling a timber problem from a footing problem

No single clue settles it, but the pattern across the house helps. These are the questions to ask.

**One door or several?** One door that sticks, with the rest of the house fine, points to that door or its frame. Several doors and windows binding at once, especially in the same part of the house, points toward the building.

**Does it change with the weather?** A door that sticks after rain and eases after a dry spell is behaving like swelling timber. Movement in the ground is slower, though on reactive clay the seasons do drive it. CSIRO describes the swelling and shrinkage as seasonal.

**Is there cracking nearby?** Cracks running from the corners of a door or window frame, cornices opening along the ceiling, and gaps between the wall and the skirting all suggest the structure has shifted. Our guides to [cracks in plaster walls](/blog/cracks-in-plaster-walls/) and [cracks in ceilings](/blog/cracks-in-ceiling/) describe what each pattern can look like.

**Is the gap uneven?** Hold a straightedge against the frame, or look at the gap between door and frame. A gap that is wider at the top than the bottom, or that tapers across the door, shows the opening has changed shape. The sticking has a geometry to it.

**Did anything change recently?** A leaking pipe, a downpipe that now discharges next to the wall, a new tree, or an automatic watering system running too long can all alter the moisture around a footing. CSIRO lists overwatering from automatic systems as a common cause of saturation.

## What the soil has to do with it

Perth is not uniform. A Perth geotechnical firm states that the most common soil type in the metropolitan area is sand, with clay sites occurring particularly in the Perth Hills. Geoscience Australia describes the Guildford Formation as predominantly silty and sandy clay, outcropping over much of the eastern Perth region. Clay swells and shrinks with moisture, and sand can be washed away if water gets under a footing. We do not assign a soil type to any suburb, and nobody should do it from a street name. A site's actual classification comes from an investigation of the site.

## What to record before anyone visits

A few notes taken over several weeks tell an engineer more than a single snapshot. This is what to gather.

1. **A list of every door and window that sticks**, with the room and which corner is binding.
2. **Photographs** of each gap and of any cracks nearby, with a tape measure or coin in frame for scale.
3. **Crack widths**, measured at the widest point, with the date. A cheap crack gauge or a ruler held against the wall works.
4. **The weather and watering pattern** at the time: after rain, in a dry spell, or while the garden system was running.
5. **Recent changes**: plumbing leaks, new paving, a tree removed or planted, drainage altered.
6. **A repeat set of photos and measurements** every two to four weeks. The change over time matters more than any one reading.

Do not fill cracks or plane doors until you have the first set of records. Fixing the symptom before you record it throws away the evidence.

## When to call an engineer

Not every sticking door needs one. These are the signs that justify the call.

- Several doors or windows are sticking in the same part of the house.
- Cracks are present beside the openings, and they are widening or lengthening.
- Frames look distorted, or a wall appears to lean or bulge.
- Cracks are wide enough to fall into CSIRO's moderate or severe categories.
- The sticking began soon after a plumbing leak, drainage change or a major tree event.
- You are buying or selling and the pattern is visible in the house.

A structural engineer looks at what is moving and whether it is still moving. ## What fixing it can involve

The remedy follows the cause. A timber problem is handled by a carpenter or builder. If moisture is the driver, the first fix is usually plumbing or drainage, and CSIRO lists repairing plumbing, grated drains and a paved apron as remedies for moisture problems. If the footings have moved, the engineer decides what, if anything, needs doing, and a builder carries it out. We do not do repair or building work.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do sticking doors always mean my house is moving?

No. Swelling timber, loose hinges and thick paint can all do it, especially when only one door is affected. The case for movement strengthens when several doors and windows stick together, cracks appear near them and the pattern changes over time.

### Why do my doors stick after rain?

Timber takes up moisture and swells, so a door that binds in a wet spell and frees up later is usually responding to humidity. On reactive clay the ground can also swell seasonally, which is why records over several weeks help separate the two.

### Is it safe to plane the door myself?

Planing a door is a cosmetic fix, and it can hide a pattern that an engineer would want to see. Take photographs and measurements first. If the door is one of several that stick, wait until the cause is understood.

### Which category of damage includes sticking doors?

CSIRO lists doors and windows sticking slightly in category 2 and sticking in category 3, with distorted frames in category 4. These categories are graded mainly by crack width, so a sticking door alone does not place a house in any of them.
