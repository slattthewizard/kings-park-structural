# Dilapidation Report in Perth: What It Records and Why It Matters

A neighbour is about to demolish, excavate for a basement or build next door, and somebody has mentioned a dilapidation report. Or you are the one about to build and a neighbour, a builder or an insurer wants the adjoining houses recorded first. Either way, the document is simple in purpose: it is a dated record of what a building looks like before the work starts, so that anything that changes afterwards can be compared against it.

This guide explains what a dilapidation report records, how it gets used, who tends to commission one, and who writes it.

## What a dilapidation report is

A dilapidation report, sometimes called a condition report or a pre-construction condition survey, is a written and photographic record of the existing state of a property. The word "dilapidation" sounds like damage, but the report is not a defects list for the owner to fix. It is a snapshot.

For a house, it usually walks through the exterior and interior and records what is already there: cracks in walls, ceilings and floors, gaps around doors and windows, cracks in render and brickwork, the state of paving, fences and retaining walls, and any existing movement.

Each item is described and photographed, with the date. A good record shows the crack, a scale beside it and where on the building it sits, so that a later reader can find the same crack and see whether it has changed.

## Why the record matters

Excavation, demolition, piling, heavy machinery and new footings near a boundary can all disturb the ground and cause vibration. After that kind of work, cracks sometimes appear, or existing ones appear to grow, and the question becomes whether the construction caused them.

Without a record, nobody can answer that. A crack that was already there looks the same as a new one, and the argument comes down to memory. With a dated record made before the work, the comparison is straightforward: it was there, or it was not.

That works in both directions. It protects the owner who finds new cracking after the work, and it protects the person doing the work from a claim about damage that was already present.

A dilapidation report records condition. It does not say what caused a crack, and it does not decide who is responsible. If cracks do appear later, the cause is a separate question. [Why Perth houses crack](/blog/why-perth-houses-crack/) covers the usual causes, which include moisture, leaks and trees as well as nearby building work.

## Who commissions one

Whether a report is formally required depends on the project and any conditions attached to it, so this guide does not say one is or is not. In general terms, the people who ask for one are:

- the owner or builder about to carry out work close to a neighbour, who wants a record of the neighbour's property before starting
- a neighbour who is worried about what is about to happen next door and wants their own record
- an insurer, or a contract, that asks for one as a condition of the project

If you are the one being asked, read the request carefully, and ask your council or your own legal adviser whether anything formal applies in your situation. Do not assume anything is required or not required.

The timing is the point. The record has to be made before the work begins. A report prepared after cracks appear tells you what the building looks like now, not what it looked like before.

## What a good report includes

A good dilapidation report has:

1. A date, and the address and the parts of the property covered.
2. Photographs, taken in a consistent order, showing each crack or defect in context.
3. A written description beside each photo, with crack width and length where measurable.
4. A note of what could not be accessed, such as a locked room or a roof space.
5. A clear statement that it is a record of condition at that date.

The crack measurements tie in with the CSIRO damage categories, which use crack width to describe how serious cracking tends to be, from hairline cracks under 0.1 mm to severe cracking of 15 to 25 mm. If you want that scale explained, see the [CSIRO guide to foundation maintenance and footing performance](https://research.csiro.au/infratech/wp-content/uploads/sites/38/2024/12/2979_FoundationMaintenanceandFootingPerformance_WCAG.pdf), which reproduces them from AS 2870-2011.

Two things to be careful about. First, a report that lists only the cracks the author thought were important is less useful than one that records everything visible, because the crack that matters later may have looked trivial at the time. Second, a vague description such as "minor cracking to the north wall" is hard to compare against later. Specific locations, widths and photos are what make the record usable.

## Building inspector or structural engineer?

Two kinds of professional can produce a dilapidation report, and they are not the same.

A building inspector inspects accessible areas and records the condition. A building inspector is a common choice for a straight record of condition.

A structural engineer can also record condition, and is the person to ask when the question is what a crack means. An engineer assesses structure: the footings, walls and loads, and what movement might mean. [Structural inspection vs building inspection](/blog/structural-inspection-vs-building-inspection/) explains the difference.

For a straight record of condition, a building inspector or an engineer can both do the job. If you already see cracking that worries you, or the nearby work is large, ask whether an engineer should be involved. The question to put to anyone you engage is what the report will include and who is taking responsibility for it.

## If cracks appear afterwards

If new cracks show up after neighbouring work, do not repair them yet. Photograph them with a scale and the date, compare against the dilapidation record, and note which cracks are new and which were already there. Then get the cause assessed. A [structural inspection](/structural-inspections/) by an independent engineer looks at what is moving and whether it is still moving, and [cracks in walls: when to worry](/blog/cracks-in-walls-when-to-worry/) is a useful first read.

Insurance can be a factor. Many home policies exclude gradual subsidence and ground movement, so check your own policy's exclusions. [Subsidence insurance in WA](/blog/subsidence-insurance-wa/) goes through the issue.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a dilapidation report?

It is a dated written and photographic record of a building's condition before nearby work such as demolition, excavation or construction begins. It records existing cracks and defects so that later changes can be compared against it.

### Does a dilapidation report prove who caused a crack?

No. It records what was there at the date of the survey. If new cracking appears, the cause is a separate question, and it may need an engineer to assess it.

### Who writes a dilapidation report?

Usually a building inspector, though a structural engineer can also record condition. An engineer is the better choice when you want to understand what a crack means as well as record it.

### When should a dilapidation report be done?

Before the work starts. A report written after cracks appear shows only what the building looks like at that point, not how it looked before.
