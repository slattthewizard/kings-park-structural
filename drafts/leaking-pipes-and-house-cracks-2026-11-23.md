# Leaking Pipes and House Cracks: Plumber First, Then the Engineer

New cracks have appeared, a patch of lawn stays wet, or the water bill has crept up for no good reason. A leaking pipe near a house can do more than waste water. It can change the ground under a footing, and the footing can move. This guide explains how that happens, why the order of the phone calls matters, and what to check on your insurance.

## How a leaking pipe moves a house

CSIRO's guide to foundation maintenance and footing performance says a leaking water, sewer or stormwater pipe near a building can cause erosion, swelling or saturation of the ground. Each of those acts differently, depending on what the ground is made of.

**Erosion.** Water that escapes from a pipe can carry soil with it. CSIRO notes that sandy soil is particularly susceptible to being washed away. Perth's most common soil is sand, according to a Perth geotechnical firm, so a leak can leave a void under or beside a footing. The footing then has less support in that spot and can settle.

**Swelling.** Reactive clay takes up water and expands. A leak that wets clay on one side of a house lifts or tilts that side relative to the dry side. Clay sites occur particularly in the Perth Hills, and the Guildford Formation, a silty clay, outcrops over much of the eastern Perth region.

**Saturation.** Wet ground carries less load. CSIRO puts the clay case plainly: "Even a minuscule leak can be enough to saturate a clay foundation." You do not need a burst main for this. A small, slow leak that runs for months is enough.

We do not say which suburb has which soil, and nobody should from a street name. The point is that a leak does different things on different ground, and only a site investigation tells you which applies to yours.

## Other water sources that do the same job

Pipes are not the only source. CSIRO also lists:

- **Downpipes** that are not connected to a stormwater system, which concentrate roof water beside the footings.
- **Automatic watering systems.** CSIRO says overwatering through misuse of these is a common cause of saturation.
- **Trees**, whose roots can lift footings or dry clay and cause shrinkage. The guide to [tree roots cracking a house](/blog/tree-roots-cracking-house/) covers that side.

If your cracks follow a leak, a blocked drain or a garden change, say so when you describe the problem. It narrows what the engineer needs to check.

## What a leak can look like from inside the house

The signs tend to be indirect, because the damage is in the ground. Common ones are:

- Cracks that appear or widen after a plumbing problem, particularly near a wet area such as a bathroom or laundry, or near where a pipe runs under the slab.
- Doors and windows that begin to stick. CSIRO's damage categories 2 and 3 mention doors and windows sticking, and category 3 mentions that pipes can fracture, which can make a bad situation worse.
- Cracks in plaster, cornices or tiles, [as described in our guide to cracks in plaster walls](/blog/cracks-in-plaster-walls/).
- A damp patch, lush or sunken ground, or a persistent smell near the house.
- A water meter that keeps ticking when every tap is off. Check the meter, not just the bill.

None of these proves a leak is the cause, and a leak is not always the cause. They are reasons to look.

## The order that makes sense: plumber first

If you suspect a leak, the sequence below usually saves time and money.

1. **Plumber first.** A plumber finds the leak and fixes it. Until the water stops, nothing else can be measured. An engineer who looks at a house with an active leak is looking at a moving target, and the report may be out of date as soon as the pipe is repaired.
2. **Stabilise and record.** Once the leak is fixed, keep a log. Take dated photos and crack-width measurements every few weeks, and note rainfall and watering. Ground that was saturated can take time to dry, and cracks may keep moving while it does.
3. **Engineer second.** An independent structural engineer then assesses whether the structure has moved, whether it is still moving, and what the cracks mean. That decision rests with the engineer, not with the plumber or a builder.
4. **Builder last.** If repair work is recommended, a builder carries it out against the engineer's scope. We do not do repair, underpinning or building work.

There are two reasons not to flip the order. An engineer cannot say whether movement has stopped while water is still entering the ground. And repairs to cracks done before the leak is fixed will crack again. CSIRO lists fixing plumbing among its remedies, along with grated drains and a paved apron at least 900 mm wide with a fall of 1:60. Those are context, not a specification for your site.

## What the engineer looks at

Engineers quote each job, and the work depends on the house. In general terms, an assessment after a leak looks at where the cracks are and how they pattern, whether doors and windows have shifted, the condition of the slab and footings that can be seen, and the likely path of the water. A visual assessment cannot see inside the ground, so the engineer may recommend further investigation. The structural engineer's report, and what is in it, is covered in [what a structural engineer's report contains](/blog/structural-engineer-report/).

The CSIRO guide also warns that diagnosis is complicated because symptoms do not simply reflect the original cause. A crack you see today may come from a leak, a tree, a drainage change or all three. That is the reason to bring in someone who looks at the whole picture.

## Insurance: read your own policy

Home insurance and ground movement are a common source of surprise, so read your own policy before you assume anything. We cannot tell you what your insurer will do, and nobody should say that a claim will or will not be paid.

As an example of standard wording, one large WA insurer's product disclosure statement dated 25 February 2021 (the current edition was not confirmed) contains a general exclusion for "Erosion, landslide, subsidence or movement" except where it is directly caused by an earthquake within a set period. Its water damage exclusions include "Gradual leakage over time" where the owner should have known of the leak. That is one document from one insurer, five years old, and it is here only to show the kind of wording that exists. Yours may be different.

What to do about it:

- **Read the exclusions** in your own policy and product disclosure statement, particularly sections on water damage, subsidence and gradual leakage.
- **Report a leak promptly.** Wording about what an owner should have known makes timing matter. Ask your insurer what they need and when.
- **Keep your records.** Plumber's invoices, photographs and dated measurements all help whoever assesses the situation.
- **Ask the insurer, not us.** Our guide to [subsidence and insurance in WA](/blog/subsidence-insurance-wa/) covers the general picture.

## When the pipe is not on your side of the boundary

Some pipes belong to the water or sewerage utility, or to a neighbour, and some are shared. If you find a leak outside your boundary, the plumber can tell you who is responsible. This article does not set out who pays for what, because that depends on the pipe and the property.

## What this guide does not tell you

It cannot tell you your leak is the cause of your cracks, or that your house is safe. CSIRO states that most cracking found in masonry walls is a cosmetic nuisance only, and also that monitoring should always be treated seriously. A crack from an old, stopped leak might never change. A crack from a live one might keep opening.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a small leak really damage a foundation?

According to CSIRO, even a minuscule leak can be enough to saturate a clay foundation, and a leak near sand can wash soil away. How much it matters depends on the ground, the footing and how long the leak runs. A site assessment is the only way to know.

### Should I call a plumber or a structural engineer first?

Call the plumber first, because the water has to stop before anyone can judge whether the structure is still moving. Once the leak is fixed, keep a record of the cracks and then ask an independent structural engineer to assess the movement.

### Will my home insurance cover foundation damage from a leak?

We cannot say, and nobody should promise it. Some policies exclude gradual leakage and ground movement, with wording that depends on what the owner knew. Read your own policy and product disclosure statement and ask your insurer directly.

### How long should I wait before getting the cracks assessed?

There is no fixed period. After the leak is repaired, ground that was saturated can take time to dry, so a few weeks of dated photos and crack measurements helps an engineer see whether movement has stopped. If cracks are widening quickly or doors are jamming, do not wait.
