# Cracks in Render: When the Coating Is the Problem and When the Wall Is

Render hides the brick or block behind it, which is the whole reason people use it and the whole reason a crack in it is hard to read. A line across the wall might be a shrinkage mark in a coating a few millimetres thick. It might also be the surface record of a wall that has moved. Patching it before you know which is the most common way to pay for the same repair twice.

This guide explains what the two kinds of cracking look like, what blown render above a window can mean, when a renderer is the right call, and when an engineer should look first.

## Two different things can crack

Rendered masonry is two layers doing different jobs. The wall behind (brick or block, held together by mortar, sitting on footings) carries the load. The render is a thin coating on the surface. Either layer can crack, and the pattern on the surface is your first clue to which.

### Cracks in the coating itself

When the render alone is the problem, cracking tends to look like a network. Fine lines spread across an area in a pattern often called map cracking or crazing, and they do not line up with any feature in the wall behind. This usually points to something in the coating, such as how it was mixed, applied or cured, or how it has aged. It is a surface issue rather than a sign that the wall is moving, though a surface crack can let water in and cause trouble later.

Because this pattern is general and depends on the product and the application, there is no width or spacing that makes it safe or unsafe. A renderer can look at the coating and tell you whether it is sound or has lost its bond with the wall.

### Cracks that follow the masonry

When the wall behind is moving, the render cracks where the wall cracked. The pattern follows the mortar joints. A stepped line running up the wall in a staircase shape, a vertical crack from the corner of a window or door, a horizontal line along a course of bricks: these trace the joints behind, because the render is too thin and brittle to stretch across a gap that opens in the masonry.

CSIRO notes that erosion and movement in masonry show as step cracking through the mortar and as vertical cracks at openings, and that older masonry has little resistance to it. That is the same pattern you see on the render surface when the coating has not hidden it. For the causes behind the movement itself, see [why Perth houses crack](/blog/why-perth-houses-crack/).

A crack in render that follows the joint pattern is not proof of anything serious. It is a reason to find out what is moving before anyone covers it up.

## Reading the pattern

| What you see | What it often points to | Who to ask first |
|---|---|---|
| Fine, random web of lines across an area, not following joints | The coating (map or craze cracking) | A renderer |
| Stepped line following mortar joints | Movement in the masonry behind | An engineer |
| Vertical crack at a window or door corner | Movement, or a problem at the opening | An engineer |
| Render pushed out or hollow above an opening | A problem at the lintel, or water behind the coating | An engineer, then a renderer |
| Crack that opens and closes with the seasons | Soil movement under the footings | An engineer |

This table is a guide to reading the pattern, not a diagnosis. Two cracks that look alike can have different causes, and one wall can have both kinds at once.

## Blown render and bulges above windows

Render that has pushed away from the wall above a window or door deserves a closer look. In many Perth brick homes the opening is spanned by a steel lintel, a beam of steel angle that carries the masonry above it. If the steel corrodes, it swells and pushes outward and upward, and the coating over it can crack, bulge or fall off in a patch directly above the opening. A rust stain bleeding through the render under the line of the lintel supports the same idea.

That is one possible cause. Water getting behind the render from another route can also blow it off the wall. The full explanation of how lintels fail, and what replacement involves, is in [rusted lintels in Perth homes](/blog/rusted-lintels-perth/). The practical point here: if the bulge is above an opening, do not simply knock off the loose render and patch it. The steel behind it may need to be seen, and an engineer decides whether that means opening the wall up.

## Using the CSIRO categories

CSIRO's guide to foundation maintenance and footing performance reproduces the damage categories from AS 2870-2011. They give you a shared language for crack width and what it usually means.

| Category | Approximate crack width | Typical damage |
|---|---|---|
| 0 Negligible | Under 0.1 mm | Hairline cracks |
| 1 Very slight | Under 1 mm | Fine cracks that do not need repair |
| 2 Slight | Under 5 mm | Noticeable, easily filled, doors and windows may stick slightly |
| 3 Moderate | 5 to 15 mm, or several cracks of 3 mm or more in one group | Some wall replacement possible, doors and windows stick, pipes can fracture |
| 4 Severe | 15 to 25 mm, also depends on the number of cracks | Extensive repair, frames distort, walls lean or bulge |

CSIRO also says most cracking found in masonry walls is a cosmetic nuisance only, and that monitoring should always be treated seriously. These categories were written for the masonry, so on a rendered wall you are measuring the surface of a coating, which can be wider or narrower than what is happening in the joint behind. Take the numbers as a starting point for the conversation, not a verdict. The wider picture is in [cracks in brick walls](/blog/cracks-in-brick-walls/).

## A renderer, or an engineer first?

Neither is always right. The question is what you are asking someone to fix.

A renderer is the right first call when the cracking looks like the coating: a random network of fine lines, no pattern that follows joints, no sticking doors, nothing at openings, and no sign of water coming through. Ask them whether the render has lost its bond with the wall, which they can often judge by tapping and listening for hollow patches.

An engineer should look first when:

- cracks follow the mortar joints, particularly in a stepped line
- there are cracks at the corners of openings
- render is bulging or lifting above a window or door
- doors and windows have started to stick, or cornices are cracking inside
- the crack has grown or opened and closed with the seasons
- the crack is wide enough to sit in the moderate or severe categories above

An engineer assesses the cause and designs what, if anything, needs to change. A builder or renderer then carries out the repair. Patching in the wrong order costs money, because if the wall is still moving a good render job will crack again.

## Do not patch before diagnosing

A fresh coat of render over a moving wall will crack again, often in the same place, and the patch can hide the evidence an engineer needs. Before anyone touches the wall:

1. Photograph each crack with a tape measure in the frame, and record the date.
2. Mark the ends of the crack with a pencil and the date, so growth can be seen.
3. Photograph the inside of the wall at the same spot.
4. Check for obvious water sources: a leaking downpipe, a broken pipe, a garden bed against the wall.
5. Ask for an assessment before agreeing to a repair.

A [structural engineer's report](/blog/structural-engineer-report/) typically covers walls, floors, slab and footings, and signs of cracking, sagging or shifting. Ask what the assessment will cover before you agree to it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are cracks in render always structural?

No. A fine, random web of cracks across the coating is often a surface issue with the render itself. Cracks that follow the mortar joints, run in a stepped line or appear at the corners of openings are more likely to reflect movement in the wall behind, and an engineer should look at them.

### Can I just fill cracks in render with sealant or patch them?

Filling a crack can seal out water for a while, but it does not tell you why the crack appeared. If the wall is still moving, the crack will reopen. Photograph and monitor it first, and get the cause diagnosed before paying for a repair.

### Why is the render bulging above my window?

It may be water getting behind the coating, or a steel lintel corroding and pushing outward. You cannot tell which from the surface. Have an engineer assess it before the loose render is removed, because the steel behind may need to be exposed.

### How do I know which CSIRO crack category I have?

Measure the widest part of the crack and compare it with the table above, noting that CSIRO's categories were written for masonry and render can widen or narrow the apparent width. Treat the category as a guide, and ask an engineer to confirm it on site.
