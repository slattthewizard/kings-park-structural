# House Extension Approval in WA: The Steps, the Timeframes and the Fees

You have a sketch of the extension, a rough idea of the budget, and no clear picture of what has to happen before a builder can dig. In Western Australia the answer is a sequence, and the order matters because a late surprise (an engineer's detail the council wanted, a planning question nobody asked) stops the clock on everything after it. This guide walks through the sequence for a house extension, using the Building Act 2011 and the Building Regulations 2012 as the source.

## Step 1: Confirm the work is outside the exemption

Some small jobs do not need a building permit. An extension is not one of them. Under Schedule 4 of the Building Regulations 2012, renovation work is exempt only if it does not affect structural soundness and does not include an increase or decrease in floor area or height. It also cannot involve underpinning or replacement of footings, or removal or alteration of an element that contributes to the support of another element.

An extension adds floor area. That alone takes it outside the exemption, so a building permit is needed. If the job is a shed or patio rather than an extension, the engineering side is covered in [engineering certificates for patios and sheds](/blog/engineering-certificate-patio-shed/).

## Step 2: Ask the council about planning approval

A building permit and planning approval are two different approvals. Planning looks at things like where the extension sits on the lot and how it fits the area. The building permit looks at whether the structure is built to the building rules.

Planning requirements vary by council and by site, and this guide does not state any planning rules, setbacks or size limits. Ring your local council, or check its website, before you pay for drawings. If planning approval is needed, ask whether it has to be in place before you lodge for the building permit, because the building drawings have to match what planning approved.

## Step 3: Choose certified (BA1) or uncertified (BA2)

A building permit application in WA comes in two forms.

| | Certified (BA1) | Uncertified (BA2) |
|---|---|---|
| Who checks the design | A registered private building surveyor, who signs the certificate of design compliance | The council's own building surveyors |
| Which buildings | Any classification | Class 1a and Class 10 only |
| Decision period | 10 business days | 25 business days |

Ask your council or building surveyor which classification your extension falls under, as that decides whether the uncertified path is open. The Building Act sets this out in section 14, and regulation 15 of the Building Regulations names the classes allowed for uncertified applications. Under the certified path, the application includes a certificate of design compliance signed by a building surveyor, the plans, and any technical certificates the surveyor relied on. Under the uncertified path, the council refers the plans to a building surveyor, who decides whether to sign.

The decision periods come from regulation 21. They can be longer if you agree in writing. City of Rockingham's published explainer states the same 10 and 25 business days.

If you want to understand the document the surveyor signs, [certificate of design compliance in WA](/blog/certificate-of-design-compliance-wa/) explains it.

## Step 4: Line up the engineering before you lodge

This is where extensions stall. A permit application needs drawings, and the drawings need structural inputs.

The footings under an extension depend on the AS 2870 site class of the ground. That class describes how reactive the soil is, from A (most sand and rock sites) up through the clay classes. Footings are designed for the class, not guessed, and replacing or underpinning existing footings is itself outside the permit exemption. How footings are designed is covered in [footing design for extensions](/blog/footing-design-for-extensions/).

The structural details also matter to the council. City of Rockingham's list of documents for an uncertified application includes a copy of structural engineer's details, designed and certified by a practising structural engineer. Expect other councils to want something similar, and ask yours what it needs before you pay for any drawings.

Where the extension joins the existing house, the engineer may need to check what the new work will load: existing walls, beams over any opening you are creating, and the footings underneath. If you are cutting a wall open to join the new room to the old, that is a [wall removal job as well](/wall-removal-engineering/). Our page on [extension engineering](/extension-engineering/) describes what an engineer looks at.

Engineers quote each job. The price depends on the house, the structure and what the council needs. No Perth engineering firm that we checked publishes a fee for extension engineering, so there is no honest figure to put here.

A note on who does what. In Western Australia, registration of structural building engineers began on 1 July 2024. After a transition period ending 1 July 2027, structural building engineering work must be done by a registered building engineer. Ask any engineer who quotes you about their registration, and check the register at wa.gov.au.

## Step 5: Lodge, and understand the clock

Once the application is lodged, the decision period starts: 10 business days for certified, 25 for uncertified, unless you agreed a longer time in writing. Two things catch owners out.

First, the clock stops if the permit authority asks for further information. A missing detail, a drawing that does not match the planning approval or an engineer's page that was left out can all trigger a request. How the stopped period is counted is set out in the legislation, and this guide does not try to paraphrase it. The practical point is that a complete application is the fastest one.

Second, the decision period is a statutory limit for the authority to decide, not a schedule for the whole project. It does not include the time you spend getting drawings, planning approval or engineering ready.

## Step 6: Fees and levies

Government charges on a building permit are set by regulation. The current figures:

| Charge | Amount |
|---|---|
| Certified building permit, Class 1 or Class 10 | 0.19% of estimated value, but not less than $121.00 |
| Uncertified building permit | 0.32% of estimated value, but not less than $121.00 |
| Building Services Levy on a permit | 0.137% of value if over $45,000; $61.65 if $45,000 or less |
| Construction Training Fund levy | 0.2% of the value of construction work including GST, payable where the value is more than $100,000 |

The value of work includes GST. As an illustration only, not a quote: on $60,000 of work, 0.19% would be $114, so the $121.00 minimum applies. On the same $60,000 the Building Services Levy would be 0.137%, because the work is over $45,000. Your own figures will differ.

Older council fee sheets still show a $110.00 minimum and a $20,000 threshold for the training levy. Those are out of date. The Construction Training Fund levy applies from $100,000 now, and the permit minimum is $121.00. Check the current table on the [WA Government building approvals page](https://www.wa.gov.au/organisation/building-and-energy/building-approvals) before you budget.

These are government charges only. They do not include the engineer, the drafter, the surveyor (on the certified path) or the builder.

## Step 7: Home indemnity insurance

Residential building work over $20,000 must be covered by home indemnity insurance. That is the builder's obligation, not yours, but it is worth asking for the certificate before you sign a contract. If your builder cannot produce it for work above that value, treat it as a red flag and ask why. The cover limits are not published on the pages this guide relies on, so ask the insurer or builder for the policy wording.

## If you also want a second storey

A second storey is a different level of structural question, because the existing walls and footings have to carry new load. [Second storey additions in Perth](/blog/second-storey-addition-perth/) goes into that. The permit steps above still apply, with more engineering in front of them.

## A short checklist

1. Confirm the work adds floor area or height, so a permit is needed.
2. Ask the council whether planning approval is required, and get it first if so.
3. Decide between the certified and uncertified paths.
4. Get the site class and structural details done by an independent structural engineer.
5. Lodge a complete application, and answer any request for further information quickly.
6. Budget the permit fee, the Building Services Levy and, over $100,000, the training levy.
7. Check the builder's home indemnity insurance before signing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need a building permit for a house extension in WA?

Yes, in almost every case. Adding floor area or height takes the work outside the building permit exemption in Schedule 4 of the Building Regulations 2012. Confirm the details with your local council or a building surveyor.

### How long does a building permit take in WA?

The statutory decision period is 10 business days for a certified application and 25 business days for an uncertified one, unless you agree to a longer time in writing. The clock stops when the authority asks for further information, so a complete application moves fastest.

### What is the difference between BA1 and BA2?

BA1 is a certified application, where a registered private building surveyor signs the certificate of design compliance. BA2 is an uncertified application, assessed by the council's own building surveyors, and it is available only for Class 1a and Class 10 buildings.

### Is planning approval the same as a building permit?

No. They are separate approvals that look at different things. Planning rules vary by council and site, so ask your local council whether planning approval is needed before you pay for building drawings.
