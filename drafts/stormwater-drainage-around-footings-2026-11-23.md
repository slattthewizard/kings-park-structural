# Drainage Around House Foundations: How Water Moves a Perth House

Walk around your house after a heavy downpour and look at where the water goes. If a downpipe empties onto the ground beside the wall, or the garden bed against the brickwork is soaked days later, you have found the most common way drainage around house foundations goes wrong. Water is rarely dramatic. It sits next to a footing, soaks in, and over a few seasons the ground under that footing changes. This guide covers how water at the footings causes movement, what the CSIRO says to do about it, and where the line sits between drainage work and engineering.

## Why water at the footings matters

A footing is only as steady as the ground under it. Sand can wash away. Clay can swell when wet and shrink when dry. Either way, a change in how much water reaches the ground beside a house can change how much support the footing gets.

CSIRO's guide, Foundation Maintenance and Footing Performance, sets out the causes of footing movement that owners can actually influence. Three of them are about water:

- **Roof water.** Downpipes that are not connected to a stormwater system concentrate water next to the footings.
- **Garden watering.** CSIRO says overwatering through the misuse of automatic watering systems is a common cause of saturation.
- **Leaking pipes.** A leaking water, sewer or stormwater pipe near a building can cause erosion, swelling or saturation. CSIRO's wording is blunt: even a minuscule leak can be enough to saturate a clay foundation.

On sand, the main worry is erosion. CSIRO notes that sandy soil is particularly susceptible to being washed away. A stream of water from a broken downpipe can scour out the ground beside a footing, leaving less under it than there should be. On clay, the worry is the seasonal swing between wet and dry, which is why a constantly wet patch on one side of a house and dry ground on the other can pull the structure in two directions.

## The three usual suspects

### Downpipes that do not go anywhere useful

Roof water adds up quickly. A downpipe that ends at ground level with no connection to the stormwater system sends all the water from its share of the roof into one spot, right beside the footing. Look for downpipes that finish in a splash block, an open pipe stub, or nothing at all. Look too for stormwater connections that were disconnected during a renovation, or a pipe crushed by a vehicle or a paving job. Cracked or blocked stormwater lines can leak below the surface where you never see them.

### Reticulation and hand watering

Automatic systems are set once and forgotten. A sprinkler that throws water against the wall, a dripper line laid along the footing, or a lawn watered daily through winter keeps one part of the ground wetter than the rest. On clay, a permanently wet strip next to the house and a drier zone further out is exactly the uneven moisture that drives swelling and shrinking. Check where each sprinkler head points and how long the system runs. A common sense rule: the ground next to the footings should not look different from the ground ten metres away.

### Leaking pipes

A slow leak does not announce itself. Signs include a patch of lawn that stays green or boggy in summer, a damp or staining area on a wall, a water meter that moves when everything is off, or a sunken patch of paving. Leaks are plumbing problems first. A licensed plumber can pressure-test and locate them. If you suspect a leak, deal with it before anyone talks about the structure, because there is no point repairing a crack while water keeps feeding the cause.

## What CSIRO lists as remedies

CSIRO's list of remedies for drainage problems is short and practical. The ones relevant here are:

1. Fix the plumbing. Repair leaking supply, sewer and stormwater pipes.
2. Install grated drains where water collects against the building.
3. Lay a paved apron around the house at least 900 mm wide, with a fall of 1:60 so water runs away from the walls.

The fall figure is worth understanding. A fall of 1:60 means the surface drops 1 unit for every 60 units of distance. Across a 900 mm apron, the outer edge sits 15 mm lower than the edge at the wall. That figure is just the ratio worked through as an illustration. The point is the direction: paving that slopes toward the wall, or sits flat, does the opposite of what you want.

One more practical point. Garden beds heaped up against the wall, mulch piled over a weep hole, or paving that has settled and now tilts inward all defeat the apron. If water reaches the footing line after rain, the apron is not working, whatever it looked like when it was laid.

## Soakwells and how close they can go

Many Perth properties dispose of roof water through soakwells, pits that let stormwater soak into the ground on site. A soakwell too close to a building can itself be a problem, because the excavation can undermine footings, and the water it releases arrives right where you do not want it.

The City of Joondalup states that soakwells must be set back from buildings and boundaries by at least the width of the soakwell, where the width is equal to or greater than the depth. The stated reason is that the excavation should not undermine footings, fences or walls. That is one council's rule. We have not found the setback rules for other Perth councils, so check with yours before any soakwell is added or moved.

## Who does what

This is where owners often lose time, so it helps to be plain about the roles.

| Problem | Who usually deals with it | What they do |
|---|---|---|
| Leaking pipe | Plumber | Locates and repairs the pipe |
| Downpipes not connected | Plumber or drainage contractor | Connects them to stormwater or a soakwell |
| Paved apron, grated drains | Landscaper or paving contractor | Builds the apron and drains |
| Reticulation misuse | You, or an irrigation installer | Resets heads and run times |
| Has the house actually moved? | Structural engineer | Assesses cracking and movement |

Drainage work is for plumbers and landscapers. It is not engineering, and a referral service like ours does not do it. What an engineer adds is a judgement on the building itself: whether movement has happened, whether it is still happening, and whether the drainage fault is the likely cause or just one of several. CSIRO makes the same point about diagnosis in general, noting that symptoms do not simply reflect the original cause. A crack by a corner with a leaking downpipe nearby may or may not be connected. Without looking at the pattern of cracking, the doors and the ground, nobody can say.

## Signs the water may already have moved something

Fixing the drainage stops new water from doing damage. It does not tell you what has already happened. Things worth noting:

- Cracks in the brickwork near the wet area, especially stepped cracks through the mortar or vertical cracks at openings
- Doors and windows that bind or stick
- Cornices pulling away from walls or ceilings
- Paving or a slab edge that has dropped or tilted
- Gaps opening between the wall and a path

If any of these sit near a drainage fault, photograph them with a tape in the frame, note the date and keep an eye on them. ## A sensible order of work

1. **Stop the obvious water.** Reconnect or extend downpipes, reset reticulation, and have any suspected leak tracked down by a plumber.
2. **Fix the ground around the house.** Reshape beds so they fall away from the wall, and consider an apron and grated drains where water collects.
3. **Record any cracking.** Date it, measure it and photograph it so you can tell later whether it changes.
4. **Get an engineer if the building shows movement.** Drainage fixes do not replace an assessment where cracking is wide, growing or paired with sticking doors.
5. **Check your insurance wording.** Many home policies exclude gradual subsidence and ground movement, and a gradual leak you should have known about can be excluded too. Read your own policy's exclusions rather than assuming. Our article on [subsidence and insurance in WA](/blog/subsidence-insurance-wa/) goes further.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can water around my foundations really crack the house?

Yes, it can contribute. Water that saturates the ground beside a footing can erode sand or make clay swell, and uneven moisture changes the support under the footing. Whether a particular crack is caused by water is something an engineer judges from the pattern of cracking and the site, not something anyone can say from a drainage fault alone.

### How wide should a paved apron around the house be?

CSIRO lists a paved apron at least 900 mm wide with a fall of 1:60 as one remedy for drainage problems. The fall carries water away from the wall instead of toward it. A landscaper or paving contractor builds it, and it is not engineering work.

### How close can a soakwell be to the house?

The City of Joondalup requires a soakwell to sit at least the width of the soakwell away from buildings and boundaries, where the width is equal to or greater than the depth. We have not found other councils' setback rules, so confirm with your own council before installing or moving a soakwell.

### Should I call a plumber or a structural engineer first?

Call a plumber first if you suspect a leak or a disconnected downpipe, since stopping the water comes before anything else. Call an engineer if you also see widening cracks, sticking doors or sagging, because only an engineer can assess whether the building has moved.
